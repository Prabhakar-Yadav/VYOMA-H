# Visual symptoms

- **Yellow between the veins while the veins themselves stay green**, giving a
  fine green net over a yellow leaf.
- Shows on the **youngest, uppermost leaves first**; older lower leaves stay
  green.
- In severe cases new leaves emerge almost white or cream.
- No spots, no lesions, no powder, no scorching.
- Appears in the same patches of a field every season, often where the soil is
  most alkaline, most calcareous, or worst drained.

# Distinguishing from wheat yellow rust

This is the confusion that matters. A farmer sees a yellow wheat leaf and
reports yellow, and yellow rust is the far more famous problem — but the
treatment for one is useless for the other.

| | Interveinal chlorosis | Yellow rust |
|---|---|---|
| **Powder** | **None.** Rubbing a finger on the leaf leaves nothing | **Orange-yellow powder** comes off on the finger — this alone settles it |
| Pattern | Yellow **between green veins**, net-like | Yellow-orange **stripes running along the leaf**, in rows |
| Which leaves | **Youngest, top** leaves first | **Lower and middle** leaves first, moving upward |
| Spread | Does not spread; static patches | Spreads plant to plant across the field over days |
| Weather | Unrelated to weather | Follows cool, humid spells |

**The single question to ask the farmer: is there orange powder that comes off
on your finger?** Powder means rust. No powder, with green veins on a yellow
leaf, means a nutrient problem.

| Also looks like | Tell it apart by |
|---|---|
| Nitrogen deficiency | Nitrogen yellows the **whole leaf evenly**, starting at the **bottom**. Iron keeps the veins green and starts at the **top**. |
| Sulphur deficiency | Sulphur also hits top leaves but yellows the whole blade evenly, not between the veins. |

# Critical treatment note

**This is not a disease. A fungicide does nothing for it.** Propiconazole or any
other rust spray applied here is money spent for no effect, and the crop keeps
declining.

**This device does not carry verified iron or manganese doses.** The correction
depends on soil pH and what is actually short — and on alkaline soils, adding
iron to the soil often fails because it locks up again immediately, so the
method matters as much as the amount. Direct the farmer to a Krishi Vigyan
Kendra with a soil test.

# Where this is common

Alkaline and calcareous soils, and waterlogged patches. Worth noting the
reversal against the rest of this corpus: iron shortage is an **alkaline** soil
problem, whereas zinc shortage is common on **acid** soils. Over-liming an acid
soil to correct acidity can create iron deficiency where none existed, which is
one reason lime rates must come from a soil test rather than a rule of thumb.
