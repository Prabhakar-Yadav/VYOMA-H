# Plant Pathology Reference — Diseases of Indian Crops

Purpose: field-usable pathogen biology for a farmer advisory device. Each entry covers what kind of
organism the pathogen is and what that implies for management, the disease cycle, survival between
seasons, entry route and incubation period, the exact weather that triggers an epidemic, spread
routes, seed-borne and soil-borne status, why varietal resistance breaks down, and why repeated use
of one chemical mode-of-action group stops working.

Scope note: this file contains biology and principles only. No pesticide product names and no doses
appear here by design. Where chemistry is discussed it is at the level of **FRAC mode-of-action
group numbers and mechanism categories**, because rotation between modes of action is a biological
principle, not a product recommendation.

Reading convention: ⚠ marks a figure that is uncertain, varies between published sources, or is
region-specific.

# PART A — THE FOUNDATIONAL PRINCIPLE

## A1. The pathogen's identity decides what works. Everything else follows from this.

This is the single most important idea in the whole corpus. A farmer who can place a disease into
the correct organism group already knows the shape of the correct answer, even before knowing the
disease name.

There are six kinds of plant pathogen, and each one responds to a **different kind of action**:

| Organism group | What it is | What management acts on it |
|---|---|---|
| **True fungi** | Multicellular organisms with chitin cell walls; reproduce by spores | **Fungicides act on fungi.** Also resistant varieties, crop rotation, sanitation, canopy and moisture management, seed treatment, biocontrol fungi and bacteria |
| **Oomycetes** (water moulds) | A separate kingdom from fungi; cellulose cell walls, swimming zoospores | **Oomycete-specific fungicide groups act on oomycetes** (FRAC 4, 21, 22, 40, 43, 45, 49, and the multi-site groups M01–M05). Also drainage, raised beds, avoiding water stagnation, resistant varieties |
| **Bacteria** | Single-celled prokaryotes multiplying in plant tissue and in water films | **Bacterial diseases are managed by cultural practice, clean planting material and resistant varieties.** Copper-based and antibiotic-class products are used as protectants; the durable control comes from clean seed, sanitation, tool hygiene, drainage, roguing, grafting onto resistant rootstock and crop rotation |
| **Viruses** | Nucleic acid in a protein coat; multiply only inside living plant cells | **Viral diseases are managed by vector management, clean and certified planting material, roguing infected plants early, weed-host removal, barrier and border crops, protected nurseries and resistant or tolerant varieties.** Sprays that reduce virus spread do so by acting on the insect vector |
| **Phytoplasmas** | Wall-less bacteria living only in the phloem, spread by phloem-feeding insects | **Managed by leafhopper/planthopper vector management, disease-free planting material, roguing, and tolerant varieties.** Hot-water or hot-air treatment of setts and suckers cleans planting material |
| **Nematodes** | Microscopic roundworms living in soil and roots | **Managed by resistant varieties, crop rotation with non-host crops, soil solarisation, organic amendments, biocontrol fungi and bacteria, nursery-bed treatment, and summer ploughing.** Nematicides act on nematodes |

### A2. Say it plainly to the farmer

- **Fungicides act on fungi.** A fungicide applied to a fungal disease — blight, blast, mildew,
  anthracnose, rust, leaf spot, wilt caused by *Fusarium*, sheath blight — is acting on the organism
  it was designed for, and the result depends on timing, coverage and mode-of-action rotation.
- **Bacterial diseases are managed by cultural practice, clean planting material and resistant
  varieties.** Bacterial wilt, bacterial leaf blight of rice, citrus canker, bacterial blight of
  pomegranate — for these the durable answers are clean seed and setts, resistant or grafted
  material, crop rotation, field sanitation, tool disinfection, drainage and roguing.
- **Viral diseases are managed by controlling the insect that carries them, by planting clean
  material, by removing infected plants early, and by growing resistant varieties.** Leaf curl,
  yellow mosaic, bunchy top, mosaic — for these the target of action is the whitefly, aphid,
  thrips, leafhopper or mealybug that moves the virus, together with the weeds and volunteer plants
  that hold it between seasons.
- **Phytoplasma diseases — little leaf, phyllody, grassy shoot, yellows — are managed the same way
  as viruses: vector management, clean setts and suckers, roguing, and tolerant varieties.**
- **Nematode problems are managed from the soil: resistant varieties, rotation with non-host crops,
  summer ploughing, solarisation, organic matter and biocontrol organisms.**

### A3. The most common and most expensive field mistake

A farmer sees leaf spots, wilting or yellowing, and applies a fungicide. If the cause is a
bacterium, a virus, a phytoplasma or a nematode, the money is spent and the crop continues to
decline — and the delay costs the window in which the correct action (roguing, vector management,
drainage, sanitation) would still have worked.

**Therefore the first question the advisory device should answer is always: what kind of organism is
this?** The management follows automatically. Three quick field discriminators:

**Bacterial signals** — water-soaked lesions that look wet and translucent when held against the
light; **lesions bounded by leaf veins giving angular shapes**; a **milky or creamy ooze** from a
cut stem suspended in clear water (the "ooze test", diagnostic for *Ralstonia solanacearum*);
**sticky, slimy exudate** drying to a crust; **rapid wilting of a green plant with no yellowing
first**; a foul smell in soft rots; spread following rain-splash, overhead irrigation, and handling
of wet plants.

**Viral and phytoplasma signals** — **mosaic, mottling, vein clearing, vein banding**; **leaf curl,
cupping, puckering and reduction in leaf size**; **stunting of the whole plant**; **yellowing in a
pattern rather than in patches**; **phyllody (flowers becoming leaf-like), little leaf, witches'
broom, excessive shoot proliferation** (these last are especially characteristic of phytoplasmas);
**absence of any spore mass, mould, pustule or ooze**; a **scattered, random distribution of
affected plants in the field following the flight of the insect vector**, or a distribution
following the edges of the field where the vector immigrates.

**Fungal and oomycete signals** — **visible growth on the lesion**: powdery white coating,
downy greyish-white growth on the leaf underside, cottony white mycelium, orange or brown pustules
releasing dust-like spores, black sclerotia like mustard seeds, pink or salmon spore masses,
concentric target-like rings in a spot. **If you can see the organism itself, it is a fungus or an
oomycete.**

**Nematode signals** — **galls or knots on roots** (root-knot), **patchy stunted areas in the
field** that appear as irregular patches rather than uniform decline, yellowing and midday wilting
that recovers at night, and **poor response to fertiliser and water in those patches**.

### A4. Fungi and oomycetes are different organisms, and this changes the chemistry

**Oomycetes** — *Phytophthora*, *Pythium*, and all the downy mildews (*Peronospora*, *Plasmopara*,
*Pseudoperonospora*, *Sclerospora*, *Peronosclerospora*, *Bremia*) — are **not true fungi**. They
belong to the Stramenopiles/Chromista, are more closely related to brown algae and diatoms than to
mushrooms, and they differ from true fungi in three ways that matter in the field:

1. **Cellulose cell walls instead of chitin**, and **no ergosterol in the membrane**. Because
   ergosterol biosynthesis is the target of the large FRAC group 3 (DMI/triazole) class,
   **DMI/triazole chemistry is built around true fungi**, while **oomycetes are addressed by their
   own mode-of-action groups**: FRAC 4 (phenylamides), 21 (QiI), 22, 40 (CAA — carboxylic acid
   amides), 43 (benzamides), 45, 49 (OSBPI), plus the multi-site protectants M01–M05.
2. **Motile, swimming zoospores with two flagella**, released from sporangia in **free water**.
   This is why oomycete diseases are diseases of **wet soil, standing water, poor drainage,
   prolonged leaf wetness and rain**. **Drainage, raised beds and broad ridges, avoidance of
   flood irrigation, and wider spacing for air movement are direct, physically-acting controls**
   because they remove the water film the zoospore needs to swim in.
3. **Thick-walled sexual oospores** that survive many years in soil and debris. Oospores are why
   rotation periods for *Phytophthora* are long and why infested soil stays infested.

**Practical statement for the farmer**: *"Downy mildew, late blight and Phytophthora rots are water
moulds. They need free water on the leaf or in the soil. Drainage, ridges, wider spacing and
morning irrigation act directly on them, and they are targeted by their own group of fungicides —
different from the ones used for powdery mildew and leaf spots."*

And the converse pairing that farmers most often get wrong:
**Powdery mildew and downy mildew have similar names and opposite biology.**

| | **Powdery mildew** | **Downy mildew** |
|---|---|---|
| Organism | **True fungus** (Ascomycota) | **Oomycete** (water mould) |
| Where the growth appears | **White powder on the UPPER leaf surface** (and both surfaces) | **Downy, greyish-purple growth on the LOWER leaf surface**, under a yellow angular patch on the upper surface |
| Lesion shape | Diffuse white patches | **Angular, bounded by leaf veins** |
| Weather | **Warm, DRY days with high humidity at night and cool nights; free water on the leaf actually inhibits spore germination**; thrives in dry weather and shade | **Cool to moderate temperature with FREE WATER on the leaf, dew, rain, fog, high humidity above 85–90 %**; needs leaf wetness |
| Survival | Cleistothecia/chasmothecia, and mycelium in dormant buds on perennials | **Oospores in soil and debris**, and systemic mycelium in perennial hosts |
| Chemistry | True-fungus mode-of-action groups, including FRAC 3 (DMI), 11 (QoI), 13, 50, and sulphur (M02) | **Oomycete mode-of-action groups** FRAC 4, 21, 40, 43, 45, 49, plus multi-site protectants |

Getting this pair right is worth more to a farmer than almost any other single diagnostic skill,
because the two look superficially similar, occur on the same crops (grape, cucurbits, onion,
crucifers), and are addressed by different chemistry and different cultural measures.

### A5. Why one chemical group stops working — resistance, in plain biology

A pathogen population contains **enormous numbers of individuals** — a single blighted potato
lesion produces hundreds of thousands of spores, and there are many generations in a season. Within
that population, rare individuals carry a **mutation that makes them insensitive to one specific
biochemical target**.

- When a **single-site** chemical is applied — one that binds one enzyme in the pathogen — the
  sensitive individuals are removed and the rare insensitive ones survive and reproduce.
- Repeat the same mode of action, and each application enriches the population in insensitive
  individuals.
- After a few seasons the insensitive type dominates, and the product that once worked well
  produces little effect. **The chemical did not weaken; the population changed.**

**The properties that make a pathogen high-risk for resistance:**
- **Many generations per season** (polycyclic diseases: downy mildews, powdery mildews, *Botrytis*,
  *Alternaria*, *Cercospora*, blast, late blight)
- **Enormous spore production and long-distance airborne dispersal**
- **Sexual recombination** generating new genotype combinations
- **Exposure to the same single-site mode of action repeatedly**

**The highest-risk pathogens in Indian agriculture** include *Phytophthora infestans*, *Plasmopara
viticola*, *Pseudoperonospora cubensis*, *Podosphaera xanthii* and *Erysiphe necator*, *Botrytis
cinerea*, *Alternaria* spp., *Magnaporthe oryzae* (rice blast), and *Cercospora* spp. ⚠ Risk
rankings are published by FRAC and are updated as monitoring data accumulate
(https://www.frac.info/).

**Mode-of-action groups and the FRAC code**
Every fungicide belongs to a **FRAC group with a number or letter code, printed on the label**. Two
products with **different trade names but the same FRAC number are the same mode of action** to the
pathogen, and alternating between them provides no rotation at all. This is the most common and
most costly misunderstanding in resistance management, and the advisory device should teach the
farmer to **read the FRAC code on the label rather than the brand name**.

Broad categories:
- **Multi-site inhibitors (FRAC M01–M09 — including copper M01, sulphur M02, dithiocarbamates M03,
  phthalimides M04, chloronitriles M05)**: act on many biochemical targets at once. Because a
  pathogen would need many simultaneous mutations to escape, **resistance to multi-site materials
  develops very slowly, and they remain effective over decades**. They are protectants applied
  before infection and they are the backbone of a rotation programme and the ideal mixing partner.
- **Single-site inhibitors (FRAC 1, 3, 4, 7, 9, 11, 12, 40, 43, 49 and others)**: act on one enzyme
  or one process. They are highly active, often systemic and curative, and they are the groups at
  risk. Examples of mechanism categories: **FRAC 1 (MBC/benzimidazoles — β-tubulin assembly)**,
  **FRAC 3 (DMI/triazoles — sterol C14-demethylation)**, **FRAC 4 (phenylamides — RNA polymerase,
  oomycetes)**, **FRAC 7 (SDHI — succinate dehydrogenase, complex II)**, **FRAC 11 (QoI/strobilurins
  — cytochrome bc1 Qo site, complex III)**, **FRAC 40 (CAA — cellulose synthase, oomycetes)**,
  **FRAC 9 (anilinopyrimidines — methionine biosynthesis)**, **FRAC 12 (phenylpyrroles — MAP
  kinase signalling)**.

**The rules that follow directly from the biology:**
1. **Rotate mode-of-action groups between applications** — a survivor of FRAC 11 meets FRAC 3 or
   FRAC 7 next, and a mutation conferring escape from one confers no escape from the other.
2. **Apply single-site chemistry in mixture with, or in alternation with, a multi-site protectant**
   — the protectant removes the individuals that survive the single-site product.
3. **Limit the number of applications of any one group per season** — commonly two to three, and
   never consecutively.
4. **Apply protectants before infection** on the weather forecast, when disease pressure is low; a
   small pathogen population contains fewer resistant individuals and generates less selection.
5. **Combine chemistry with resistant varieties, rotation, sanitation and canopy management**, so
   that chemistry carries less of the total load and therefore applies less selection pressure.
6. **Note that cross-resistance operates within a group**: once resistance to one FRAC 11 compound
   appears, the whole FRAC 11 group is generally affected, regardless of brand.

The identical logic applies to insecticides through **IRAC** groups and to herbicides through
**HRAC** groups. The principle is universal: **rotate the mode of action, not the brand name.**

### A6. Why varietal resistance breaks down — the boom-and-bust cycle

A resistant variety carries one or a few **resistance genes (R genes)**, each of which recognises
one specific molecule (an **effector**) produced by the pathogen. This is called **vertical,
race-specific or qualitative resistance**, and it is complete while it lasts.

The pathogen population, however, is variable. Individuals that have **lost or altered that one
effector** are no longer recognised, and they multiply freely on the resistant variety. Because the
resistant variety is planted over a very large area, the selective advantage is enormous:

1. **Boom** — the new resistant variety is released and adopted widely; disease disappears.
2. **Selection** — a rare pathogen genotype (a new **race**, **pathotype** or **strain**) that
   escapes recognition multiplies unchecked on that vast uniform area.
3. **Bust** — within a few seasons the new race dominates and the variety becomes susceptible.
4. A new R gene is deployed, and the cycle repeats.

**Documented Indian and global examples:**
- **Wheat stem rust *Puccinia graminis* f. sp. *tritici* race Ug99** and its lineage, which
  defeated the widely deployed **Sr31** resistance.
- **Wheat yellow (stripe) rust *P. striiformis*** races in the north-western plains and Himalayan
  foothills, which shift repeatedly and have defeated successive Yr genes.
- **_Fusarium oxysporum_ f. sp. *cubense* Tropical Race 4 (TR4)** on banana, which overcame the
  Cavendish resistance that had replaced Gros Michel after Race 1.
- **_Xanthomonas oryzae_ pv. *oryzae* pathotypes** in rice, which have overcome individual Xa
  genes, leading to the deployment of **pyramided combinations such as Xa21 + xa13 + xa5**.
- ***Magnaporthe oryzae*** (rice blast) races, which shift within a few seasons.
- ***Meloidogyne incognita*** overcoming the tomato **Mi-1** gene, and the separate phenomenon that
  **Mi-1 resistance ceases to be expressed above about 28 °C soil temperature** — a temperature
  effect rather than a genetic breakdown, but with the same practical result in Indian summers.
- **Begomoviruses**, where **recombination between virus species and the acquisition of
  betasatellite DNA** generate new variants that overcome tolerance, as in the successive
  Cotton leaf curl epidemics in north-west India.

**What makes resistance durable:**
- **Horizontal, quantitative, polygenic or "slow-rusting/adult plant" resistance** — many genes
  each of small effect. It is partial rather than complete, so it looks less impressive, but the
  pathogen must accumulate many changes simultaneously to overcome it, which is far less likely.
  Wheat **Sr2, Lr34/Yr18/Pm38 and Lr46** are the classic durable adult-plant resistance genes.
- **Gene pyramiding** — several R genes stacked in one variety, so a pathogen individual must carry
  several simultaneous changes.
- **Varietal diversity across a landscape** — growing several different varieties, or varietal
  mixtures, so that no single recognition specificity covers the whole area and the pathogen
  population is never presented with one uniform target.
- **Reducing the total pathogen population** through rotation, sanitation, clean seed and
  appropriately timed protectants, so that fewer individuals exist in which a rare escape mutation
  can arise.

**Practical statement for the farmer**: *"A resistant variety works because it recognises the
disease. Over time the disease changes so it is no longer recognised, and the variety needs to be
replaced. Growing more than one variety, changing varieties periodically, and keeping the overall
disease level low with rotation and clean seed all make each variety last longer."*

### A7. The disease triangle — the frame for every entry below

A disease appears only when **three things coincide**: a **susceptible host**, a **virulent
pathogen**, and **favourable environment**, over **time**. Remove or weaken any one and the disease
declines. This gives four independent families of action, and a good recommendation usually
combines several:

- **Act on the host** — resistant or tolerant varieties, healthy vigorous plants, balanced
  nutrition (excess nitrogen produces soft, succulent tissue that is more readily colonised;
  adequate potassium and calcium strengthen cell walls and are associated with lower disease),
  correct plant spacing and grafting onto resistant rootstock.
- **Act on the pathogen population** — clean and certified seed and planting material, seed
  treatment, roguing and removal of infected plants, destruction of crop residue and stubble,
  removal of collateral and weed hosts, crop rotation with non-hosts, soil solarisation, tool and
  implement disinfection, and protectant or systemic chemistry applied at the correct timing.
- **Act on the environment** — drainage and raised beds so that soil water does not stagnate,
  wider spacing and pruning for air movement and light penetration, **irrigating early in the day
  and by furrow or drip rather than overhead in the evening so that leaves dry before nightfall**,
  mulching to prevent rain-splash of soil-borne spores onto lower leaves, and shade or windbreak
  management.
- **Act on the vector** — for viruses and phytoplasmas this is the principal lever: barrier and
  border crops, insect-proof nursery netting, reflective and coloured mulches, sticky traps, weed
  host removal, host-free periods, and conservation of the vector's natural enemies.

### A8. Monocyclic and polycyclic — why some diseases need one action and others need many

- A **monocyclic** disease completes **one infection cycle per season**. The inoculum comes from the
  soil, the seed or the debris at the start, and there is little or no spread from plant to plant
  during the season. Examples: *Fusarium* wilts, bacterial wilt, smuts, root-knot nematode on an
  annual crop. **For these, everything depends on what is done before and at planting** — clean
  seed, seed treatment, resistant variety, rotation, soil management. Once the crop is infected,
  the season's outcome is largely already determined.
- A **polycyclic** disease completes **many infection cycles per season**, each new lesion producing
  spores that start further infections within days. Examples: late blight, downy mildews, powdery
  mildews, rusts, blast, *Alternaria*, anthracnose, sheath blight. **For these, the epidemic
  accelerates exponentially and early action is worth many times late action.** A protectant
  applied before the first lesions appear, based on the weather forecast, holds the epidemic at a
  low level; the same product applied after the epidemic is running struggles against a population
  that is doubling every few days.

**Practical statement for the farmer**: *"For wilts, smuts and nematodes, the crop is decided before
sowing — choose the variety, the seed and the field. For blights, mildews, rusts and spots, the crop
is decided by whether you act in the first week of favourable weather or the third."*

# PART B — PATHOGEN GROUPS

## B1. *Xanthomonas* — bacterial blights, spots and cankers

### What kind of organism, and what that means
***Xanthomonas* is a BACTERIUM** — aerobic, Gram-negative, non-spore-forming, rod-shaped,
1–2 × 0.8–1.0 µm, with a single polar flagellum (monotrichous). Colonies are circular, convex,
whitish-yellow to straw-yellow and opaque; the yellow pigment (xanthomonadin) gives the genus its
name.
(eagri PATH 272 Lecture 01 — http://eagri.org/eagri50/PATH272/lecture01/0011.html)

**Because it is a bacterium, management is built on cultural practice, clean planting material and
resistant varieties.** Bacteria multiply in water films and in plant sap, enter only through
natural openings and wounds, and are moved by water and by hands and tools. The actions that work
are therefore: **clean seed and setts, resistant or pyramided-resistance varieties, field
sanitation and stubble destruction, drainage and avoidance of flooding, avoidance of wounding,
disinfection of knives and secateurs, removal of weed and collateral hosts, balanced nitrogen, and
roguing.** Copper-based protectants and antibiotic-class materials are used as surface protectants
and to slow spread; the durable control comes from the cultural and genetic measures.

### Key species in India
| Pathogen | Disease | Crop |
|---|---|---|
| *X. oryzae* pv. *oryzae* | Bacterial leaf blight (BLB), kresek | Rice |
| *X. oryzae* pv. *oryzicola* | Bacterial leaf streak | Rice |
| *X. citri* subsp. *citri* | Citrus canker | Citrus |
| *X. campestris* pv. *campestris* | Black rot | Crucifers |
| *X. axonopodis* pv. *punicae* | Bacterial blight / oily spot ("telya") | Pomegranate |
| *X. euvesicatoria* / *X. vesicatoria* | Bacterial spot | Tomato, chilli |
| *X. axonopodis* pv. *malvacearum* | Angular leaf spot / black arm | Cotton |

### Bacterial leaf blight of rice — the reference case

**Symptoms.** Usually noticed at heading, but it can occur earlier. Seedlings in the nursery show
circular yellow spots at the margin that enlarge and coalesce, drying the foliage. The **"kresek"
symptom** appears in seedlings **1–2 weeks after transplanting**: the bacteria **enter through the
cut wounds in the clipped leaf tips**, become systemic, and kill the entire seedling. In grown
plants, **water-soaked, translucent lesions appear near the leaf margin**, enlarge in length and
width with a **wavy margin**, and **turn straw-yellow within a few days**, eventually covering the
lamina, which turns white or straw-coloured. **Milky or opaque dew drops containing bacterial
masses form on young lesions in the early morning** and dry to a white encrustation.
(eagri PATH 272 Lecture 01 — http://eagri.org/eagri50/PATH272/lecture01/0011.html)

**The field diagnostic — the ooze test.** *"If the cut end of the leaf is dipped in water, it
becomes turbid because of bacterial ooze."* This costs nothing, needs only a glass of clear water,
and distinguishes a bacterial disease from a fungal one in about a minute. **This single test is
one of the highest-value items a farmer advisory can teach.**
(eagri PATH 272 Lecture 01)

**Favourable conditions.**
- **Clipping the tips of seedlings at transplanting** — this creates the wound the bacterium needs.
- **Heavy rain, heavy dew, flooding, deep irrigation water.**
- **Severe wind** (wind-driven rain wounds leaves and moves bacteria) and **temperature of
  25–30 °C**.
- **Excessive nitrogen, especially late top-dressing.**
(eagri PATH 272 Lecture 01 — http://eagri.org/eagri50/PATH272/lecture01/0011.html)

**Disease cycle and survival.** The pathogen **survives in soil, in infected stubble, and on
collateral hosts *Leersia* spp., *Plantago major*, *Paspalum distichum* and *Cynodon dactylon*
(bermuda/hariali grass)**. It **spreads through irrigation water and through rain storms**.
Infected seed is a comparatively minor source, because the bacterial numbers fall rapidly and the
bacteria die during seed soaking.
(eagri PATH 272 Lecture 01)

**How it enters the plant.** Through **hydathodes at the leaf margin and tip** (the natural water
pores from which guttation drops form — which is why the disease starts at the margin and why
guttation-favouring humid nights promote it), and through **wounds**, especially clipped leaf tips
and wind-whipped leaves. It then colonises the **xylem** and moves systemically, which is why
kresek kills the whole seedling.

**Incubation period.** Symptoms appear roughly **7–14 days** after infection under favourable
conditions; kresek shows within **1–2 weeks of transplanting**. ⚠

**Management (positively stated).**
- **Burn the stubble** to remove the surviving population.
- **Use an optimum dose of fertiliser**; split nitrogen and keep late top-dressing moderate.
- **Transplant without clipping the seedling tips** — retain intact leaf tips so the bacterium has
  no wound to enter.
- **Maintain shallow, intermittent irrigation with good drainage**; keep water from an affected
  field out of a healthy field.
- **Remove weed and collateral hosts** from bunds and channels.
- **Grow resistant cultivars** — TNAU lists IR 20 and TKM 6; modern practice deploys **pyramided
  resistance combining Xa21 + xa13 + xa5**, which is far more durable than any single gene.
(eagri PATH 272 Lecture 01 — http://eagri.org/eagri50/PATH272/lecture01/0011.html)

**Why resistance breaks down.** *X. oryzae* pv. *oryzae* populations in India are divided into
**pathotypes** distinguished by which Xa genes they overcome. The bacterium uses **TAL effectors**
that bind specific promoter sequences in the rice genome to switch on susceptibility genes; a small
change in the effector, or in the plant's promoter, changes the recognition. When one Xa gene is
deployed across a large area, pathotypes that escape it are strongly selected. **Pyramiding
several Xa genes into one variety requires the pathogen to change several things at once and is
therefore far more durable.** ⚠ Pathotype frequencies differ markedly between Indian states and
shift over years.

### Bacterial leaf streak of rice — *X. oryzae* pv. *oryzicola*
Fine **translucent streaks form BETWEEN the veins** (interveinal), enlarge lengthwise, turn brown,
and under humid conditions **bacterial ooze forms small yellow band-like exudates** on the lesion
surface. **The diagnostic contrast with BLB: streak lesions are interveinal, linear and
translucent when held to the light, and begin anywhere on the blade; blight lesions begin at the
leaf margin or tip and have a wavy margin.** *X. o.* pv. *oryzicola* enters mainly through
**stomata** rather than hydathodes, which is why it produces streaks in the leaf blade rather than
marginal blight. Management is the same package: burn stubble, optimum fertiliser, avoid clipping
seedling tips, avoid flooded conditions, remove weed hosts, grow resistant cultivars.
(eagri PATH 272 Lecture 01 — http://eagri.org/eagri50/PATH272/lecture01/0012.html)

### Citrus canker — *X. citri* subsp. *citri*
Raised, corky, **crater-like lesions with a water-soaked yellow halo** on leaves, twigs and fruit;
lesions are visible on **both leaf surfaces**, which separates canker from scab. It enters through
**stomata and through wounds**, and **wounds made by the citrus leaf miner (*Phyllocnistis
citrella*) dramatically increase infection** — so **leaf miner management is a canker management
measure**. Spread is by **wind-driven rain**, which is the dominant dispersal route; **the practical
implication is that windbreaks reduce canker**, and that operations should be avoided when the
canopy is wet. Survives in **lesions on twigs and in leaf litter**. Managed by clean nursery
material, pruning and burning of cankered twigs, windbreaks, leaf miner management, copper
protectants, and tolerant rootstock/scion combinations. ⚠

### Black rot of crucifers — *X. campestris* pv. *campestris*
The textbook **seed-borne, hydathode-entering** bacterium. Characteristic **V-shaped yellow lesions
opening from the leaf margin inward, with blackened veins** inside the V. It enters through
**hydathodes at the leaf margin**, colonises the **xylem**, and blackens the vascular bundles —
cut the petiole and the black ring of vessels is diagnostic. **Seed-borne**, surviving on and in
seed for **2–3 years**, and surviving in **infected crucifer debris in soil for about 1–2 years**
(it survives as long as the host debris persists, and declines rapidly once the debris decomposes).
Managed by **hot-water seed treatment (about 50 °C for 20–30 minutes — a treatment that works
because the bacterium is killed at a temperature the seed tolerates)**, certified clean seed,
**3-year rotation out of crucifers**, removal of cruciferous weeds (wild mustard, shepherd's
purse), avoidance of overhead irrigation, and resistant varieties. ⚠ Hot-water temperatures and
times differ by crucifer species; the treatment must be precise.

### Pomegranate bacterial blight ("telya") — *X. axonopodis* pv. *punicae*
Water-soaked, **oily-looking dark spots** on leaves and fruit, with **fruit cracking along the
lesion**, which destroys market value. Enters through **stomata and wounds**; spreads by
**rain splash, wind-driven rain, and contaminated pruning tools**. Survives in **infected twigs,
fallen leaves and fruit on the orchard floor**. Managed by **removing and destroying infected plant
parts, disinfecting pruning tools between plants, controlling the bahar (forcing the rest period)
so that the susceptible flush avoids the monsoon, orchard sanitation, windbreaks, and copper
protectants**. The **bahar regulation** point is important and specific: shifting to **hasta bahar**
so that fruit development falls outside the heavy-rain window is a management decision made months
ahead. ⚠

## B2. Rusts — *Puccinia*, *Uromyces*, *Hemileia*, *Phakopsora*

### What kind of organism, and what that means
Rusts are **true fungi (Basidiomycota, order Pucciniales)** and they are **obligate biotrophs** —
they can grow only on a living host and cannot be cultured on artificial media. This has three
direct consequences:
1. **They cannot survive as saprophytes in dead debris.** They must have a living green host at all
   times, so **the "green bridge" — volunteer plants, self-sown crop, off-season crops in the hills,
   ratoon and weed hosts — is the whole survival story.** Destroying the green bridge is the
   fundamental cultural control.
2. **They produce enormous numbers of wind-borne urediniospores** and are **polycyclic**, so
   epidemics accelerate very fast once conditions are right. Early protectant action is worth many
   times late curative action.
3. **They are highly variable, with defined physiological races**, so resistant varieties come under
   continuous selection pressure.

Fungicides act on fungi, so rusts are addressed by true-fungus mode-of-action groups — notably
**FRAC 3 (DMI/triazoles)**, **FRAC 11 (QoI/strobilurins)**, **FRAC 7 (SDHI)**, and the multi-site
protectants **FRAC M03 (dithiocarbamates)**.

### Wheat rusts — the three rusts and the Indian "Puccinia path"

| | **Black / stem rust** | **Brown / leaf rust** | **Yellow / stripe rust** |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pathogen | *Puccinia graminis* f. sp. *tritici* | *P. triticina* (*P. recondita*) | *P. striiformis* f. sp. *tritici* |
| Pustule colour | **Dark reddish-brown** | **Orange** | **Bright yellow to orange-yellow** |
| Pustule shape | **Oval to spindle-shaped**, erupting through the epidermis with **tattered host tissue** around it | **Small, circular blisters** | **Arranged in LINEAR ROWS as stripes** |
| Where | Mainly **stem, leaf sheaths, both leaf surfaces** | Mainly **upper leaf surface of blades**; also sheaths, glumes, awns | Mainly **leaves**, less on sheaths and stem |
| Favourable temperature | **15–20 °C with high humidity, November–December** | **15–20 °C with high humidity, November–December** | **Below 10 °C** |
| Indian zone | Central and peninsular India | Throughout | **North-western plains and Himalayan foothills** |
(eagri PATH 272 Lecture 03 — http://eagri.org/eagri50/PATH272/lecture03/001.html, /002.html, /003.html)

**Symptom detail.** Stem rust pustules are **dusty and release spores readily when touched**; as
infection advances **teliospores form in the same pustule and the colour changes from rust to
black**. Heavy pustule load **weakens stems and causes lodging**. Leaf rust: **orange spores are
easily dislodged and cover clothing, hands and implements** — a useful diagnostic and a reminder
that **workers and equipment move spores between fields**. Because inoculum is blown in, **symptoms
appear on the upper leaves first** — this "top-down" pattern is diagnostic of an airborne
introduced epidemic, as opposed to soil- or debris-borne diseases which start on lower leaves.
**Heavy infection reaching the flag leaf shortens the grain-fill period and produces small
kernels** — the flag leaf is the yield-critical leaf, so flag-leaf protection is the priority
timing.
(eagri PATH 272 Lecture 03 — http://eagri.org/eagri50/PATH272/lecture03/001.html and /002.html)

**Disease cycle in India — and why the barberry story does not apply here.**
*"In India, all these rusts appear in the wheat growing belt during the Rabi crop season. Uredosori
turn into teliosori as summer approaches. The inoculum survives in the form of uredospores /
teliospores in the hills during the off season on self-sown crop or volunteer hosts, which provide
an excellent source of inoculum. In India, the role of the alternate host (*Berberis*) is not there
in completing the life cycle."*
*"Uredospores and dormant mycelium survive on stubbles and straws and also on weed hosts and
self-sown wheat crops. Wind-borne uredospores from the hills are lifted due to cyclonic winds and
infect the crop in the plains during the crop season."*
(eagri PATH 272 Lecture 03 — http://eagri.org/eagri50/PATH272/lecture03/003.html)

This is the celebrated **"Puccinia path"**: rust survives the hot Indian summer on **self-sown and
volunteer wheat in the cool Nilgiri and Palni hills in the south and in the Himalayan foothills in
the north**, and is carried by winds down onto the plains each rabi season. The practical
consequences are large:
- **The plains crop is re-infected from outside every year**, so stubble destruction in the plains
  alone does not prevent the epidemic; **eliminating off-season self-sown wheat in the hills is a
  national-scale control measure.**
- **Rust arrives from a predictable direction at a predictable time**, which is why India operates a
  **national rust surveillance and forecasting system through ICAR-IIWBR, Karnal**
  (https://iiwbr.icar.gov.in/) and why farmers in the north-west can be warned in advance.
- Because the alternate host (*Berberis*, barberry) plays no role in India, **the sexual cycle is
  largely absent and new races arise mainly by mutation and by migration from outside**, notably
  the Ug99 lineage tracked from East Africa.

**Full life cycle for reference** (*P. graminis* is macrocyclic and heteroecious): **urediniospores**
(the repeating, dikaryotic, wind-borne stage that drives the epidemic) → **teliospores** (thick-
walled overwintering stage, black) → **basidiospores** → infect **barberry (*Berberis*)**, where
**pycnia/spermagonia form as raised orange spots on the upper leaf surface**, producing **small
amounts of honeydew that attract insects** for cross-fertilisation, and **aecia form on the lower
leaf surface, yellow and bell-shaped, extending as far as 5 mm from the surface** → **aeciospores**
infect wheat again. **The barberry stage matters because it is where sexual recombination generates
new races** — which is precisely why barberry eradication was historically effective in North
America and Europe, and why its absence from the Indian cycle changes the race dynamics here.
(eagri PATH 272 Lecture 03 — http://eagri.org/eagri50/PATH272/lecture03/001.html)

**Management (positively stated).**
- **Grow resistant varieties** — TNAU lists PBW 343, PBW 550, PBW 17; current recommendations
  change with race surveys, so the operative advice is to **follow the current ICAR-IIWBR varietal
  recommendation for your zone**, which is updated as races shift.
- **Prefer varieties carrying durable adult-plant resistance** (Sr2, Lr34/Yr18, Lr46) alone or in
  combination with race-specific genes.
- **Practise mixed cropping and varietal diversity** so that no single resistance specificity covers
  the whole area.
- **Apply an optimum, not excessive, dose of nitrogenous fertiliser** — lush dense canopies hold
  humidity and favour rust.
- **Sow on time.** Late-sown wheat is exposed at a susceptible stage when the rust inoculum has
  built up, and yellow rust in particular hits late-sown crops harder.
- **Destroy volunteer and self-sown wheat** during the off-season to break the green bridge.
- **Protect the flag leaf** — this is the timing that converts to yield.
(eagri PATH 272 Lecture 03 — http://eagri.org/eagri50/PATH272/lecture03/003.html)

### Other important rusts in India
- ***Puccinia arachidis*** — **groundnut rust**. Orange-brown pustules on the **lower leaf surface**
  that **do not have a yellow halo** (distinguishing them from late leaf spot, *Phaeoisariopsis
  personata*, with which rust almost always occurs as the "rust–late leaf spot complex").
  Favoured by **temperature 20–25 °C with relative humidity above 85 % and prolonged leaf
  wetness**. In India *P. arachidis* is known **only in the uredinial stage** — no teliospore stage
  functions and no alternate host is involved — so it survives entirely on **volunteer groundnut,
  in crop debris and on other *Arachis* species**, and moves by wind. ⚠
- ***Puccinia sorghi*** — **common rust of maize**; **cinnamon-brown, elongated pustules on both
  leaf surfaces**, favoured by **cool, moist conditions, 16–23 °C with high humidity**. Alternate
  host *Oxalis*.
- ***Puccinia polysora*** — southern rust of maize, favoured by hotter conditions (25–28 °C).
- ***Uromyces* spp.** — rusts of pulses: *U. viciae-fabae* on pea, lentil, faba bean; *U.
  appendiculatus* on French bean; *U. ciceris-arietini* on chickpea. Favoured by **17–22 °C with
  high humidity and leaf wetness**; survive on **crop debris and volunteer hosts**; several are
  **autoecious**, completing the whole cycle on the one host.
- ***Hemileia vastatrix*** — **coffee leaf rust**, the historically most destructive rust of a
  perennial crop. **Orange-yellow powdery lesions on the LOWER leaf surface** with corresponding
  **chlorotic patches on the upper surface**. It is an **obligate biotroph entering exclusively
  through the STOMATA on the lower leaf surface**, which is why **spray coverage must reach the
  lower leaf surface** and why upper-surface-only deposition performs poorly. Germination
  **requires free water (dew or rain) on the leaf for 6 or more hours in darkness**, at
  **21–25 °C optimum**; the fungus **is inhibited by light during germination**, so **night-time
  leaf wetness is the trigger**. Spread is by **wind and by rain splash, and notably on the hands,
  clothing and equipment of pickers moving through the plantation**. It survives between seasons as
  **mycelium in living leaves that remain on the tree** and on volunteer/wild coffee. In India it is
  managed by **resistant varieties (the arabica selections carrying *Hemileia*-resistance genes
  developed by the Central Coffee Research Institute), shade regulation to reduce leaf wetness
  duration, canopy management and removal of infected leaves, balanced nutrition, and protectant
  copper sprays timed to the pre-monsoon and post-monsoon flushes**. Resistance breaks down because
  *H. vastatrix* has **many physiological races (over 50 described)**. ⚠
- ***Phakopsora pachyrhizi*** — Asian soybean rust; **tan to reddish-brown angular lesions with
  volcano-shaped uredinia on the lower leaf surface**; favoured by **18–26 °C with 10 or more hours
  of leaf wetness**. It survives on **volunteer soybean and on alternative legume hosts including
  *Pueraria* (kudzu)**.
- ***Melampsora*, *Puccinia purpurea*** (sorghum rust), and ***Puccinia kuehnii*/*P. melanocephala***
  (sugarcane rusts) also occur in India.

### Why rust resistance breaks down and how to slow it
Rust populations contain enormous numbers of individuals; a single field produces billions of
urediniospores in a season, and the spores travel between regions and continents. A rare individual
that escapes recognition by a deployed R gene multiplies without competition on a uniform host
area. **Ug99** (*P. graminis* f. sp. *tritici* race TTKSK and its lineage) is the textbook example:
it overcame **Sr31**, which had been deployed in a very large share of world wheat area. The
counters are **gene pyramiding, deployment of durable adult-plant resistance, varietal diversity
across the landscape, periodic varietal replacement guided by race surveys, and reduction of the
overall inoculum load by destroying the green bridge**.

### Mode-of-action rotation for rusts
Rusts are polycyclic with very large populations and are therefore **medium-to-high risk for
fungicide resistance**. **FRAC 11 (QoI/strobilurin)** resistance in cereal pathogens is typically
conferred by a **single point mutation (G143A) in the cytochrome b gene**, which appears readily and
confers **complete cross-resistance across the whole FRAC 11 group** — this is why alternating
between two different strobilurin brands provides no protection. **FRAC 3 (DMI/triazole)**
resistance is **quantitative and builds gradually** through accumulation of several changes, so
sensitivity erodes slowly rather than collapsing. The practical programme is: **rotate FRAC 3, 7
and 11 between applications; use each group at most twice per season and never consecutively;
partner single-site products with a multi-site protectant (FRAC M03); and apply on forecast, before
pustules are widespread.**
(https://www.frac.info/)

## B3. *Rhizoctonia solani* — sheath blight, banded leaf and sheath blight, damping-off, root rots

### What kind of organism
A **true fungus** (Basidiomycota; sexual/teleomorph stage *Thanatephorus cucumeris*), and an
unusual one: it is a **sterile, soil-inhabiting fungus that produces no asexual spores (no conidia)
at all**. It spreads as **mycelium growing through soil and across plant surfaces**, and it
survives as **sclerotia**. This single fact explains most of its epidemiology:
- **There is no wind-borne spore shower**, so it does not arrive from a distant field. **It comes
  from your own soil, your own crop residue, or your own irrigation water.**
- **It spreads by contact** — mycelium bridging from tiller to tiller and from plant to plant —
  which is why **close planting and dense canopies are so strongly favourable**.
- **Sclerotia float**, so **irrigation water carries them across and between fields**.
(eagri PATH 272 Lecture 01 — http://eagri.org/eagri50/PATH272/lecture01/005.html)

**Fungicides act on fungi, so *Rhizoctonia* is addressed by true-fungus mode-of-action groups** —
notably **FRAC 7 (SDHI)**, **FRAC 11 (QoI)**, **FRAC 2 (dicarboximides)**, **FRAC 1
(benzimidazoles)** and **FRAC 3 (DMI)**. Alongside chemistry, the highest-value actions are
**spacing, nitrogen moderation, water management, organic amendment, deep summer ploughing and
biocontrol**.

### Anastomosis groups — why "*Rhizoctonia solani*" behaves differently on different crops
*R. solani* is a **species complex divided into anastomosis groups (AG-1 to AG-13)**, defined by
whether hyphae of two isolates will fuse. Different AGs have different host preferences and
different behaviour:
- **AG-1 IA** — rice sheath blight, maize banded leaf and sheath blight, soybean aerial blight
- **AG-1 IB** — web blight of beans and lettuce bottom rot
- **AG-2-2** — root rot of sugarbeet, brown patch of turf
- **AG-3** — potato black scurf and stem canker
- **AG-4** — damping-off and root rot of a very wide range of seedlings
This matters practically because **a rotation crop that is a poor host for the AG present in your
field is an effective rotation, while a rotation crop that hosts the same AG is not.** ⚠ AG
determination requires a laboratory; the field proxy is crop history.

### Rice sheath blight — the reference case

**Symptoms.** The fungus affects the crop **from tillering to heading**. **Initial symptoms appear
on the leaf sheaths near the water level** — this is the signature, and it tells the farmer exactly
where to look. **Oval, elliptical or irregular greenish-grey spots** form on the sheath; as they
enlarge the **centre becomes greyish-white with an irregular blackish-brown or purple-brown
border**. Lesions on the upper parts of the plant **extend rapidly and coalesce, covering entire
tillers from the water line to the flag leaf**. Several large lesions on a sheath kill the whole
leaf, and in severe cases all leaves of a plant are blighted. Infection extends to the inner
sheaths and kills the plant. **Older plants are highly susceptible.** Plants heavily infected at
early heading and grain filling **produce poorly filled grain, especially in the lower part of the
panicle**.
(eagri PATH 272 Lecture 01 — http://eagri.org/eagri50/PATH272/lecture01/005.html)

**Pathogen structures.** Septate mycelium, **hyaline when young, yellowish-brown when old**, with
the characteristic **right-angle branching with a constriction and a septum near the branch point**.
It produces **large numbers of spherical brown sclerotia** — these look like small brown mustard
seeds sitting on the sheath and floating on the water surface, and they are the diagnostic
structure.
(eagri PATH 272 Lecture 01)

**Favourable conditions.**
- **High relative humidity (96–97 %)**
- **High temperature (30–32 °C)**
- **Closer planting**
- **Heavy doses of nitrogenous fertilisers**
(eagri PATH 272 Lecture 01 — http://eagri.org/eagri50/PATH272/lecture01/005.html)

Note that this is a **hot, wet, dense-canopy disease** — the exact opposite of the cool conditions
favouring rice blast — so the two major rice diseases are favoured by different weather and a
season that suppresses one may promote the other.

**Survival — exact figures.**
*"The pathogen can survive as sclerotia or mycelium in dry soil for about 20 months but for
5–8 months in moist soil. Sclerotia spread through irrigation water. The fungus has a wide host
range."*
(eagri PATH 272 Lecture 01 — http://eagri.org/eagri50/PATH272/lecture01/005.html)

This pair of numbers is unusually useful, and it is counter-intuitive: **sclerotia last far longer
in dry soil (about 20 months) than in moist soil (5–8 months)**, because in moist soil the resident
microbial community attacks and degrades them. The practical inference is that **keeping soil
biologically active and moist between crops, and adding organic matter, actively shortens the
survival of the inoculum** — an ecological control rather than a chemical one.

**How it reaches and enters the plant.** Sclerotia float to the water surface and **lodge against
the sheath at the waterline**, germinate, and the mycelium **penetrates directly through the
cuticle using infection cushions and lobate appressoria**, and also enters through **stomata**. It
does not need a wound. **Incubation is short — lesions appear within 2–3 days of infection and
sclerotia form within about 2 weeks** ⚠, which makes this a fast polycyclic disease within a
season.

**Management (positively stated).**
- **Grow resistant varieties** — TNAU lists Mansarovar, Swarna Dhan and Pankaj. ⚠ Complete
  resistance to sheath blight is uncommon; most available material is partially resistant, and
  cultural measures carry much of the load.
- **Apply organic amendments** — **neem cake at 150 kg/ha or farmyard manure at 12.5 t/ha**. These
  work by stimulating the soil microbial community that degrades sclerotia.
- **Avoid the flow of irrigation water from infected fields to healthy fields** — sclerotia travel
  on water, so bunding and independent inlets matter.
- **Deep ploughing in summer and burning of stubble** — buries and destroys sclerotia.
- **Widen the spacing** and avoid excessive plant density so the canopy dries and contact spread is
  slowed.
- **Moderate the nitrogen dose and split it**; supply adequate potassium and silicon, both of which
  are associated with lower sheath blight.
- **Practise alternate wetting and drying rather than continuous deep flooding**, which lowers the
  humidity at the sheath and reduces sclerotial movement.
- **Apply *Pseudomonas fluorescens*** — soil application at 2.5 kg/ha 30 days after transplanting
  (mixed with 50 kg farmyard manure or sand), and foliar spray at 0.2 % at the boot leaf stage and
  10 days later. *Trichoderma harzianum* and *T. viride* are also effective antagonists.
(eagri PATH 272 Lecture 01 — http://eagri.org/eagri50/PATH272/lecture01/005.html)

### Banded leaf and sheath blight of maize — *R. solani* f. sp. *sasakii* (AG-1 IA)
The same fungus on maize, producing **characteristic alternating light and dark concentric bands**
on the sheath and leaf — the "banded" appearance — progressing upward from the lower sheaths and
reaching the ear, where it causes ear rot. Favoured by **high humidity above 90 %, temperature
28–32 °C, dense planting and high nitrogen**, and it is a serious problem in the humid maize belts
of the north-east, the Tarai and peninsular India. Managed by **removal of the lower 2–3 infected
leaf sheaths, wider spacing, balanced nutrition, deep ploughing, rotation with non-host crops, and
*Trichoderma*/*Pseudomonas* application**.
(ICAR-IIMR — https://iimr.icar.gov.in/)

### Damping-off and seedling blights
*R. solani* (with *Pythium* spp. and *Fusarium* spp.) causes **pre-emergence damping-off** (seed
and seedling killed before emergence, seen as gappy stands) and **post-emergence damping-off**
(seedling topples at a **water-soaked, constricted, brown lesion at the collar/soil line**).
*Rhizoctonia* typically produces a **firm, dry, sunken, brown collar lesion**, whereas *Pythium*
(an oomycete) produces a **soft, watery, mushy rot** — a useful field discriminator because the two
are addressed by different chemistry groups. Favoured by **dense nursery sowing, excess watering,
poor drainage, heavy shade, cool damp conditions and high soil organic residue**. Managed by
**raised nursery beds 15 cm high, well-drained light soil, soil solarisation of the nursery bed for
3–6 weeks in summer under transparent polythene, thin sowing, seed treatment with *Trichoderma
viride* or *T. harzianum*, avoiding overwatering, and providing free air movement**.

## B4. *Macrophomina phaseolina* and *Sclerotium rolfsii* — the dry-weather soil fungi

### B4a. *Macrophomina phaseolina* — charcoal rot, dry root rot, stem and root rot

**What kind of organism.** A **true fungus** (Ascomycota, Botryosphaeriaceae), soil-borne and
seed-borne, with the sclerotial state historically called ***Rhizoctonia bataticola***. It survives
as **microsclerotia** and produces **pycnidia** containing conidia.
(eagri PATH 272 Lecture 12 — http://eagri.org/eagri50/PATH272/lecture12/001.html)

**Pathogen structures.** Dark brown, septate mycelium **showing constrictions at the hyphal
junctions**. **Sclerotia are minute, dark black, 110–130 µm in diameter** — so small and numerous
that infected tissue looks as if it has been dusted with **charcoal or soot**, which is the origin
of the name and the field diagnostic. **Pycnidia are dark brown with a prominent ostiole**; conidia
are hyaline, elliptical and single-celled.
(eagri PATH 272 Lecture 12 — http://eagri.org/eagri50/PATH272/lecture12/001.html)

**Symptoms (sesame reference case).** **Yellowing of the lower leaves, followed by drooping and
defoliation.** The stem near ground level shows **dark brown lesions** and the **bark at the collar
region shreds**. **Sudden death of plants occurs in patches** — the patchy distribution is
characteristic and reflects soil inoculum distribution and soil moisture. In grown plants the stem
near soil level carries **large numbers of black pycnidia**. **The stem can be easily pulled out,
leaving the rotten root portion in the soil** — this "pulls away at the collar, root stays behind"
test is diagnostic and free. Infection spreading to pods causes them to **open prematurely with
shrivelled black immature seeds**. Rotten root and stem tissues contain **large numbers of minute
black sclerotia**, and sclerotia occur on infected pods and seeds.
(eagri PATH 272 Lecture 12 — http://eagri.org/eagri50/PATH272/lecture12/001.html)

**Favourable conditions — this is a HEAT and DROUGHT-STRESS disease.**
- **Day temperature of 30 °C and above** (optimum around **30–35 °C**, tolerating up to 40 °C)
- **Prolonged drought followed by copious irrigation**
(eagri PATH 272 Lecture 12 — http://eagri.org/eagri50/PATH272/lecture12/001.html)

This is the key epidemiological insight and it inverts the usual expectation. **Most fungal
diseases need moisture; *Macrophomina* needs a stressed, hot, dry plant.** The fungus colonises the
roots quietly for weeks and expresses itself as sudden collapse when the plant comes under water
stress or heat stress, especially at flowering and pod filling. **The practical control is
therefore irrigation management: maintaining even soil moisture and avoiding the drought-then-flood
cycle removes the trigger.** In chickpea this is **dry root rot**, a rising problem in central India
as terminal-heat stress increases; in soybean, sorghum, maize, cotton, sunflower and pulses it is
**charcoal rot**.

**Disease cycle.**
*"The fungus remains dormant as sclerotia in soil as well as in infected plant debris in soil. The
infected plant debris also carries pycnidia. The fungus primarily spreads through infected seeds
which carry sclerotia and pycnidia. The fungus also spreads through soil-borne sclerotia. The
secondary spread is through the conidia transmitted by wind and rain water."*
(eagri PATH 272 Lecture 12 — http://eagri.org/eagri50/PATH272/lecture12/001.html)

**Both seed-borne AND soil-borne** — which means both seed treatment and soil/field management are
needed. **Microsclerotia survive in soil and debris for 3–15 years** ⚠ (commonly cited as up to
about 4 years in the plough layer, and longer in dry soils and inside undecomposed residue), so
rotation alone rarely eliminates it.

**Host range.** Exceptionally wide — **over 500 plant species**: sesame, soybean, chickpea, cowpea,
mungbean, urdbean, groundnut, sorghum, maize, pearl millet, cotton, sunflower, castor, jute,
potato, and many more. This breadth is why **rotation is difficult and why crop choice must be
combined with moisture management.**

**Entry.** Microsclerotia germinate in response to root exudates, and hyphae **penetrate the root
epidermis directly or through natural openings and wounds**, then colonise the **xylem**, blocking
water movement — which is why the symptoms are those of water stress and why water-stressed plants
collapse first.

**Management (positively stated).**
- **Maintain even soil moisture through the reproductive stage** — avoid prolonged drought followed
  by heavy irrigation; irrigate lightly and frequently rather than heavily and rarely.
- **Sow at the recommended time** so that pod filling avoids the peak terminal heat.
- **Apply farmyard manure or green leaf manure at 10 t/ha, or neem cake at 150 kg/ha** — organic
  matter improves soil water-holding capacity, supports antagonistic microflora, and directly
  shortens microsclerotial survival.
- **Treat seed with *Trichoderma viride* at 4 g/kg** — a biological seed treatment that colonises
  the rhizosphere ahead of the pathogen.
- **Use clean, healthy seed from a disease-free crop**, because the fungus is seed-borne.
- **Rotate with cereals or other less-susceptible crops** where practicable, and **practise deep
  summer ploughing** to expose sclerotia to solar heating.
- **Grow tolerant varieties** and avoid the most susceptible ones in heat-prone fields.
- **Maintain adequate potassium**, which is associated with improved plant water relations and
  lower charcoal rot.
(eagri PATH 272 Lecture 12 — http://eagri.org/eagri50/PATH272/lecture12/001.html)

### B4b. *Sclerotium rolfsii* — collar rot, stem rot, southern blight

**What kind of organism.** A **true fungus** (Basidiomycota; teleomorph *Athelia rolfsii*), soil-
borne, producing **abundant coarse white mycelium** and **sclerotia that look exactly like mustard
seeds** — round, 1–2 mm, **starting white and maturing to tan and then dark brown**. The white,
fan-like mycelial growth spreading over the stem base and out onto the soil surface, dotted with
mustard-seed sclerotia, is one of the most unmistakable diagnoses in plant pathology.

**Symptoms.** **Sudden wilting and death of an apparently healthy plant**, with a **soft, brown rot
at the collar (soil line)** covered in **white cottony mycelium bearing mustard-seed-like
sclerotia**. Affected plants pull up easily; the bark at the collar shreds. Occurs in **patches**
in the field, often following the line of previous crop residue.

**Crops in India.** Groundnut (stem rot), chickpea and lentil (collar rot), tomato, chilli, brinjal,
potato, sugarbeet, betelvine, turmeric and ginger (rhizome rot complex), sunflower, and many
vegetables. **Host range over 500 species.**

**Favourable conditions.** **High temperature 25–35 °C (optimum around 30 °C)**, **high soil
moisture with high humidity**, **acidic soils (pH 3.0–5.0 favour it)**, and — critically —
**abundant undecomposed organic matter or crop residue on and just below the soil surface**, which
the fungus uses as a food base to build up before attacking the crop. **Newly incorporated green
residue immediately before sowing is a classic trigger.**

**Survival.** **Sclerotia survive in the top 5–8 cm of soil for several years** ⚠ (commonly cited as
up to about 3–5 years, and longer in dry soil). They are **killed by prolonged submergence** — which
is why **flooding a field, or rotating with paddy, is an effective control** — and they are
**concentrated in the surface layer**, which is why **deep ploughing that buries sclerotia below
about 20 cm strongly suppresses the disease**, since the fungus is aerobic and needs oxygen and a
surface food base.

**Entry.** Direct penetration of the stem base and collar, assisted by **oxalic acid and
cell-wall-degrading enzymes** that macerate the tissue ahead of the hyphae. It does not require a
wound.

**Management (positively stated).**
- **Deep summer ploughing to bury sclerotia** below the surface layer where they cannot function.
- **Soil solarisation** with transparent polythene for 4–6 weeks in the hot months, which raises
  surface soil temperature enough to kill sclerotia.
- **Allow crop residue to decompose fully before sowing**, and avoid sowing immediately into freshly
  incorporated green matter.
- **Rotate with paddy or flood the field**, which kills sclerotia by anaerobiosis.
- **Raise the soil pH toward neutral with lime** where soils are acidic.
- **Apply *Trichoderma harzianum* or *T. viride* and *Pseudomonas fluorescens*** as seed treatment
  and as a soil application with farmyard manure — *Trichoderma* parasitises *Sclerotium* sclerotia
  directly and is one of the best-documented biocontrol interactions.
- **Practise earthing-up away from the collar**, avoid piling wet soil and mulch against the stem,
  and keep irrigation water off the collar region.
- **Rogue and remove affected plants together with the surrounding soil** to prevent sclerotia from
  being added to the field.

### B4c. *Sclerotinia sclerotiorum* — white mould, stem rot
A **true fungus** producing **large, irregular, black sclerotia (3–10 mm, resembling rat droppings)
inside hollow stems and on the plant surface**, with dense **white cottony mycelium**. It differs
from *Sclerotium rolfsii* in that it is a **cool, wet-weather pathogen (15–25 °C with prolonged
leaf wetness and dense canopy)**, and it has an **airborne phase**: sclerotia in the top few
centimetres of soil germinate to form **apothecia (small tan mushroom-like cups)** that shoot
**ascospores into the air**, which land on **senescing petals** and use them as the food base to
infect the stem. This **"petal infection" route** is why **the flowering period is the critical
window** in mustard/rapeseed, and why **dense canopies that trap fallen petals against wet stems**
are the high-risk situation. Important on **mustard/rapeseed (stem rot), French bean, cabbage,
cauliflower, carrot, potato and sunflower** in India. **Sclerotia survive 3–8 years in soil.** ⚠
Managed by **wider spacing and avoiding excessive nitrogen so the canopy stays open, deep ploughing
to bury sclerotia below germination depth, rotation with cereals (which are non-hosts), avoiding
irrigation during flowering, and *Coniothyrium minitans* or *Trichoderma* as sclerotial
parasites**.

## B5. Phytoplasmas — little leaf, phyllody, grassy shoot, yellows, witches' broom

### What kind of organism, and what that means
Phytoplasmas are **bacteria without a cell wall** — pleomorphic prokaryotes in the class
Mollicutes, formerly called "mycoplasma-like organisms (MLOs)". They live **exclusively inside the
phloem sieve tubes** of the plant and inside the body of their insect vector, and they **cannot be
cultured on ordinary artificial media**.
(eagri PATH 272 Lecture 12 — http://eagri.org/eagri50/PATH272/lecture12/008.html)

In sugarcane grassy shoot, ultrathin sections of infected phloem show **spherical bodies of
300–400 nm diameter and filamentous bodies**, confirming the wall-less pleomorphic structure.
(eagri PATH 272 Lecture 06 — http://eagri.org/eagri50/PATH272/lecture06/009.html)

**Because a phytoplasma has no cell wall, lives inside the phloem, and is delivered by an insect,
it is managed exactly as a virus is: by managing the leafhopper or planthopper vector, by planting
disease-free setts, suckers and seedlings, by roguing infected plants promptly, by removing weed
and collateral hosts, and by growing tolerant varieties.** Hot-water and hot-air treatment of
planting material is an additional and very effective tool that is available for phytoplasmas
because they are heat-sensitive.

### The symptom family — how to recognise a phytoplasma disease
Phytoplasmas disturb the plant's hormone balance, producing a distinctive and highly recognisable
symptom family that is different from anything a fungus produces:
- **Phyllody** — floral parts transformed into **green leafy structures**; the flower is rendered
  sterile.
- **Virescence** — greening of petals.
- **Little leaf** — drastic **reduction in leaf size**, with leaves crowded together.
- **Witches' broom / excessive proliferation** — abnormal branching, **numerous thin shoots from one
  point**, loss of apical dominance.
- **Grassy shoot** — numerous lanky, chlorotic tillers giving a grass-like clump.
- **General yellowing, stunting, shortened internodes, sterility.**
- **No spore mass, no mould, no pustule, no ooze** — nothing visible of the organism itself.

**Practical statement for the farmer**: *"If the flowers have turned into little green leaves, or
the leaves have become very small and crowded, or the plant has thrown out a broom of thin shoots,
this is not a disease that a fungicide addresses. Remove and destroy those plants, manage the
leafhoppers, and use clean planting material next season."*

### Sesame phyllody — the reference case
(eagri PATH 272 Lecture 12 — http://eagri.org/eagri50/PATH272/lecture12/008.html)

**Symptoms.** Starts with **vein clearing of leaves**. The disease manifests mostly at the
**flowering stage**, when **floral parts are transformed into green leafy structures which grow
profusely**, and **the flower is rendered sterile**. The **veins of the phylloid structure are
thick and prominent**. The plant is **stunted with reduced internodes and abnormal branching**.

**Vector and transmission — with exact numbers.**
- Transmitted by the **jassid / leafhopper *Orosius albicinctus*** (= *O. orientalis*).
- **Optimum acquisition feeding period of the vector: 3–4 days.**
- **Inoculation feeding period: 30 minutes.**
- **Incubation (latent) period of the pathogen in the leafhopper: 15–63 days.**
- **Incubation period in the sesame plant: 13–61 days.**
- **Nymphs are incapable of transmitting the phytoplasma.**
- **Vector population is higher during summer and lower during the winter months.**
(eagri PATH 272 Lecture 12 — http://eagri.org/eagri50/PATH272/lecture12/008.html)

These numbers carry a strong practical message. The **long latent period in both insect and plant
(2 weeks to 2 months)** means that **by the time symptoms appear, the vector has been active for a
month or more, and further infections are already incubating invisibly**. Roguing visible plants
therefore always lags the real infection; **the effective action is early-season vector management
and clean, weed-free surroundings, applied before symptoms are seen.** The long acquisition period
of 3–4 days, in contrast, means that a vector must feed for days on an infected plant to become
infective — so **removing infected plants promptly genuinely reduces the number of infective
leafhoppers**, unlike the situation with non-persistently transmitted viruses.

**Alternate and collateral hosts** — the pathogen has a wide host range and survives on
**_Brassica campestris_ var. *toria*, *Brassica rapa*, *Cicer arietinum* (chickpea), *Crotalaria*
sp., *Trifolium* sp., and *Arachis hypogaea* (groundnut)**, which serve as the source of inoculum.
(eagri PATH 272 Lecture 12)

**Management (positively stated).**
- **Remove all reservoir and weed hosts.**
- **Grow sesame away from cotton, groundnut and grain legumes**, which host the phytoplasma and the
  vector.
- **Rogue out infected plants periodically**, taking them out of the field entirely.
- **Manage the leafhopper vector**, especially in the early crop and during the summer peak.
- **Sow at the recommended time** so the susceptible flowering stage avoids the vector peak.
(eagri PATH 272 Lecture 12 — http://eagri.org/eagri50/PATH272/lecture12/008.html)

### Sugarcane grassy shoot disease (GSD)
(eagri PATH 272 Lecture 06 — http://eagri.org/eagri50/PATH272/lecture06/009.html)

**Symptoms.** Appears **about two months after planting**. Characterised by **numerous lanky tillers
from the base of affected shoots**. Leaves become **pale yellow to completely chlorotic, thin and
narrow**. Plants appear **bushy and "grass-like" due to reduced internode length and premature,
continuous tillering**. Affected clumps are **stunted with premature proliferation of axillary
buds**. **Cane formation rarely occurs**; if canes form, they are thin with short internodes and
**aerial roots at the lower nodes**, and the **buds are papery and abnormally elongated**.

**Disease cycle.**
- **Primary spread is through diseased setts and through cutting knives.** The knife point is
  important and actionable: **the same blade that cuts a diseased clump carries the phytoplasma
  into the next healthy sett**, so **disinfecting the cutting knife between clumps is a real control
  measure**.
- **Secondary transmission is by aphids: *Rhopalosiphum maidis*, *Melanaphis sacchari* and
  *Melanaphis indosacchari*.** ⚠ Note that this is an unusual case — most phytoplasmas are
  leafhopper-transmitted, and aphid transmission of GSD as reported in the TNAU notes differs from
  much of the international literature, which attributes GSD spread principally to setts and to
  leafhopper/aphid complexes.
- **Sorghum and maize serve as natural collateral hosts.**
(eagri PATH 272 Lecture 06 — http://eagri.org/eagri50/PATH272/lecture06/009.html)

**Management (positively stated) — including the exact heat treatments.**
- **Eradicate diseased clumps as soon as symptoms are seen.**
- **Select setts only from a disease-free nursery area**, never from an affected block.
- **Pre-treat healthy setts with HOT WATER at 52 °C for 1 hour before planting.**
- **Or treat setts with HOT AIR at 54 °C for 8 hours.**
- **Manage the aphid vectors on the crop.**
- **Maintain a three-tier seed nursery programme** so that certified, heat-treated, disease-free
  seed cane flows to commercial fields each year.
(eagri PATH 272 Lecture 06 — http://eagri.org/eagri50/PATH272/lecture06/009.html)

**Why hot-water treatment works and why it is so valuable.** Phytoplasmas (and the ratoon stunting
bacterium *Leifsonia xyli* subsp. *xyli*, and sugarcane grassy shoot together) are **inactivated at
temperatures the dormant sett survives**. A precise, time-controlled heat treatment therefore
**cleans the planting material** — addressing the primary inoculum route directly. This is the
single most powerful intervention available for a vegetatively propagated crop, because **in
sugarcane, banana, potato, ginger, turmeric and citrus, the planting material itself is the main
way disease moves from season to season and from district to district.**

### Brinjal little leaf
Caused by a phytoplasma ('*Candidatus* Phytoplasma trifolii' and related groups), transmitted by
the **leafhopper *Hishimonus phycitis*** (and *Amrasca biguttula biguttula* is also implicated ⚠).
Symptoms: **leaves become drastically reduced in size, pale, soft and crowded**, internodes
shorten, the plant becomes **bushy with excessive shoot proliferation**, **flowers become green and
leaf-like (phyllody) and the plant becomes sterile**, producing no fruit or small hard fruit. There
is **no cure for an infected plant** — the actionable measures are **roguing infected plants as
soon as they are seen, managing the leafhopper vector, removing solanaceous weed hosts (*Solanum
nigrum*, *Datura*, *Physalis*), avoiding continuous brinjal cropping and ratooning, and raising
seedlings under insect-proof net**. ⚠

### Other important Indian phytoplasma diseases
- **Coconut root (wilt) disease** — Kerala; ⚠ the phytoplasma association has been debated, with
  the disease long treated as of uncertain aetiology; current evidence supports a phytoplasma.
  Managed by removal of severely affected palms, healthy seedlings, balanced nutrition and
  irrigation.
- **Sandal spike disease** — sandalwood; transmitted by leafhoppers; managed by roguing.
- **Napier grass stunt, sesame phyllody, groundnut witches' broom, papaya dieback/yellow crinkle,
  citrus greening's phytoplasma look-alikes.**
- **Important distinction:** **Citrus greening / Huanglongbing (HLB) is caused by '*Candidatus*
  Liberibacter asiaticus', which is a WALLED bacterium restricted to the phloem — not a
  phytoplasma.** It is transmitted by the **Asian citrus psyllid *Diaphorina citri*** in a
  **persistent, propagative** manner. Practically the management is the same family of measures —
  **certified disease-free nursery plants raised under insect-proof structures, psyllid management,
  prompt removal of infected trees, and control of the alternate host *Murraya koenigii* (curry
  leaf) near orchards** — but the organism should be named correctly. The characteristic symptom is
  **asymmetric blotchy mottle on the leaf** (the yellowing pattern is not mirrored across the
  midrib), which distinguishes HLB from zinc deficiency, whose mottle is symmetric.

## B6. *Fusarium* — vascular wilts, root rots, and grain moulds

### What kind of organism, and what that means
***Fusarium* is a TRUE FUNGUS** (Ascomycota, Nectriaceae). It produces three spore/structure types
that between them explain its whole epidemiology:
- **Macroconidia** — **1–5 septate, hyaline, thin-walled, falcate (canoe- or sickle-shaped) with
  tapering ends**. The canoe shape is the identifying character under a microscope.
- **Microconidia** — hyaline, thin-walled, spherical or elliptical, single- or two-celled; produced
  abundantly and moved in the xylem sap, which is how the fungus travels up the plant.
- **Chlamydospores** — **dark-coloured and thick-walled resting spores**, the survival structure.
(eagri PATH 272 Lecture 13 — http://eagri.org/eagri50/PATH272/lecture13/001.html)

**Fungicides act on fungi, so *Fusarium* is addressed by true-fungus mode-of-action groups** —
notably **FRAC 1 (benzimidazoles)**, **FRAC 3 (DMI)**, **FRAC 7 (SDHI)**, **FRAC 11 (QoI)**, and
seed-treatment chemistry including **FRAC 7 carboxin-type materials**. But for a **vascular wilt the
fungus lives inside the xylem, protected from anything applied to the leaf surface**, so the
decisive measures are those applied **before and at planting**: **resistant varieties, grafting onto
resistant rootstock, clean seed and seed treatment, long rotation, soil solarisation, organic
amendment and biocontrol**. Fusarium wilts are **monocyclic** — the outcome of the season is largely
determined at sowing.

### *Formae speciales* — one fungus species, many host-specific forms
*Fusarium oxysporum* is a species complex divided into **_formae speciales_ (f. sp.)**, each of
which attacks essentially **one crop or a narrow group of crops**. This is enormously important
practically: **a *Fusarium* wilt in tomato will not move into the neighbouring maize, and a rotation
with a non-host crop genuinely works** — provided the rotation crop is not a host of that same
forma specialis.

| Forma specialis | Disease | Crop |
|---|---|---|
| f. sp. *vasinfectum* | Fusarium wilt | Cotton |
| f. sp. *lycopersici* | Fusarium wilt (races 1, 2, 3) | Tomato |
| f. sp. *ciceris* | Wilt (races 0, 1A, 2, 3, 4, 5) | Chickpea |
| f. sp. *udum* | Wilt | Pigeonpea (redgram) |
| f. sp. *cubense* | Panama disease (Races 1, 2, and **TR4**) | Banana |
| f. sp. *melonis*, *niveum*, *cucumerinum* | Wilts | Muskmelon, watermelon, cucumber |
| f. sp. *cepae* | Basal rot | Onion |
| f. sp. *zingiberi* | Yellows | Ginger |
| *F. verticillioides* (*moniliforme*) | Stalk rot, ear rot, **fumonisin** producer; bakanae of rice | Maize, rice |
| *F. graminearum* | Fusarium head blight / scab; **deoxynivalenol (DON)** producer | Wheat, barley, maize |
| *F. solani* | Root and collar rots, dry rot | Many crops, potato |

### Cotton Fusarium wilt — the reference case
(eagri PATH 272 Lecture 13 — http://eagri.org/eagri50/PATH272/lecture13/001.html)

**Symptoms.** The disease affects the crop at all stages. The earliest symptoms appear on
**seedling cotyledons, which turn yellow and then brown**; the **base of the petiole shows a brown
ring**, followed by wilting and drying. In older plants the first symptom is **yellowing of the leaf
edges and the area around the veins — discoloration starts from the margin and spreads towards the
midrib**. Leaves lose turgidity, turn brown, droop and drop. **Symptoms begin on the OLDER leaves at
the base and progress upward to the younger leaves** — this bottom-to-top progression is
characteristic of a soil-borne vascular wilt and distinguishes it from an airborne foliar disease,
which starts at the top. Defoliation may be complete, **leaving the stem alone standing in the
field**. **Partial (one-sided) wilting** — only one portion of the plant affected — is common and is
another vascular-wilt signature. The **taproot is stunted with fewer laterals**.

**The decisive diagnostic — vascular browning.** *"Browning or blackening of vascular tissues is the
other important symptom; black streaks or stripes may be seen extending upwards to the branches and
downwards to lateral roots... In transverse section, a discoloured ring is seen in the woody tissues
of the stem."*
**Cut the stem across and look for the brown ring in the wood.** This is the free, immediate field
test that separates a **vascular wilt** from a **root rot** (where the root system rots but the
vascular ring is not discoloured up the stem) and from **bacterial wilt** (where the cut stem
releases milky ooze in water). Combining the **cut-stem ring test** with the **ooze test** resolves
the great majority of wilt diagnoses in the field with no equipment at all.
(eagri PATH 272 Lecture 13 — http://eagri.org/eagri50/PATH272/lecture13/001.html)

**The toxin.** The fungus produces the **vivotoxin fusaric acid**, which is **partially responsible
for wilting**. This is why the plant wilts before the vessels are fully blocked, and why symptoms
can appear ahead of extensive colonisation.

**Favourable conditions.**
- **Soil temperature of 20–30 °C**
- **Hot and dry periods followed by rains**
- **Heavy black soils with an alkaline reaction**
- **Increased doses of nitrogen and phosphatic fertilisers**
- **Wounds caused by the root-knot nematode *Meloidogyne incognita* and by grubs of the ash weevil
  *Myllocerus pustulatus***
(eagri PATH 272 Lecture 13 — http://eagri.org/eagri50/PATH272/lecture13/001.html)

**The nematode–wilt disease complex.** The nematode point is one of the most valuable in the whole
corpus. **Root-knot nematode feeding creates wounds and physiological changes in the root that let
*Fusarium* in, and the two together cause far more wilt than either alone.** A field where wilt
appears despite a "resistant" variety is very often a field with a root-knot problem, because
**nematode infection can break Fusarium wilt resistance**. **Therefore managing root-knot nematode
is a Fusarium wilt management measure**, and a wilt problem that resists explanation should prompt
a look at the roots for galls.

**Disease cycle and survival.**
*"The fungus can survive in soil as a saprophyte for many years and chlamydospores act as resting
spores. The pathogen is both externally and internally seed-borne. The primary infection is mainly
from dormant hyphae and chlamydospores in the soil. The secondary spread is through conidia and
chlamydospores which are disseminated by wind and irrigation water."*
(eagri PATH 272 Lecture 13 — http://eagri.org/eagri50/PATH272/lecture13/001.html)

- **Both seed-borne (externally and internally) AND soil-borne.**
- **Chlamydospores survive in soil for many years** — commonly cited as **up to 10 years or more**
  even in the absence of a host, because *F. oxysporum* also lives saprophytically on organic matter
  and colonises the roots of non-host plants without causing disease. ⚠ This is why **rotation
  reduces but does not eliminate** Fusarium wilt, and why **once a field is infested it is
  effectively infested for the long term** — making resistant varieties and clean fields the
  primary strategy.

**How it enters.** Chlamydospores germinate in response to root exudates; hyphae **penetrate the
root tip and the points where lateral roots emerge, and enter through wounds**. The fungus reaches
the **xylem**, and microconidia are then **carried upward in the transpiration stream**, germinating
at vessel end-walls and repeating the process — which is how the fungus climbs the plant rapidly
with very little tissue growth. Vessel blockage by mycelium, spores, gels and tyloses, together with
fusaric acid, produces the wilt. **Incubation is typically 2–4 weeks from root infection to visible
wilting** ⚠, and is shorter in warm soil.

**Management (positively stated).**
- **Grow disease-resistant varieties** — TNAU lists Varalakshmi, Vijay, Pratap, Jayadhar and Verum
  among *G. hirsutum* and *G. barbadense* types. Resistant varieties are the backbone.
- **Treat acid-delinted seed** with an appropriate seed-treatment fungicide or with *Trichoderma
  viride*.
- **Remove and burn infected plant debris after deep summer ploughing during June–July.**
- **Apply increased doses of potash with a balanced dose of nitrogen and phosphorus.** Potassium
  nutrition is consistently associated with lower wilt.
- **Apply heavy doses of farmyard manure or other organic manures** — organic matter supports the
  antagonistic soil microflora that suppresses *Fusarium*.
- **Follow mixed cropping and rotation with non-host plants** — effective because of the
  forma-specialis host specificity.
- **Manage root-knot nematode** so that the wounding/predisposition route is closed.
(eagri PATH 272 Lecture 13 — http://eagri.org/eagri50/PATH272/lecture13/001.html)

### Fusarium wilt of banana — Panama disease and TR4
*Fusarium oxysporum* f. sp. *cubense*. **Race 1** destroyed the 'Gros Michel' banana industry
globally in the mid-20th century; the industry replaced it with **Cavendish**, which is resistant to
Race 1. **Tropical Race 4 (TR4)** overcomes Cavendish resistance and is now the defining threat to
world banana production; **it has been confirmed in India (first reported in Bihar and Uttar
Pradesh, and subsequently detected in several states)**. ⚠ Distribution within India continues to be
mapped by ICAR-NRCB, Tiruchirappalli (https://nrcb.icar.gov.in/).

**Symptoms.** **Yellowing of the older (lower) leaves progressing upward, starting at the leaf
margin**; leaves collapse at the petiole and **hang down around the pseudostem forming a "skirt"**
while remaining attached; **longitudinal splitting of the pseudostem base**; and internally
**reddish-brown to dark vascular discoloration visible on cutting the pseudostem or the corm** —
again the cut-and-look test.

**Why it is so difficult, and what follows.**
- **Chlamydospores survive in soil for 30 years or more** in the absence of a host. ⚠ This makes an
  infested field effectively permanently infested for banana.
- It spreads on **infected suckers and planting material, on soil adhering to implements, footwear,
  vehicle tyres and animals, and in surface drainage and irrigation water**.
- Therefore the management is **exclusion and sanitation before anything else**: **plant only
  tissue-culture-derived, certified disease-free planting material**; **never move suckers from an
  affected plantation**; **establish footbaths and vehicle wash points at the plantation entrance**;
  **fence off and isolate infected patches rather than ploughing through them**; **destroy infected
  mats in place and avoid moving the soil**; **manage drainage so water does not run from an
  infected block to a clean one**; **rotate with paddy where feasible**; and **grow resistant
  cultivars and improved somaclonal/TC selections as they become available**. Ginger, turmeric and
  other rotations are used, but **the decisive control is clean planting material plus exclusion.**

### Fusarium wilt of chickpea and pigeonpea
- ***F. oxysporum* f. sp. *ciceris*** on chickpea causes **early wilt (at seedling stage, killing
  plants outright) and late wilt (at flowering/podding, with drooping of petioles, rachis and
  leaflets, and a dull green colour)**. Internally, the **root and lower stem show brown-black
  vascular discoloration when split longitudinally** — a longitudinal split rather than a
  transverse cut is the standard chickpea test. Favoured by **soil temperature 25 °C and above,
  dry soil, and late sowing**; **sowing early, into cooler soil, is a recognised escape strategy**.
  ⚠ Eight races (0, 1A, 1B/C, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6) are described, and race distribution differs by
  region, which is why a variety resistant in one state may wilt in another.
- ***F. udum*** on pigeonpea causes wilt typically **from the flowering stage onward**, with
  **partial (one-sided) wilting** and a **purple-black band on the stem extending upward from the
  base**, with **internal browning of the xylem**. Favoured by **17–25 °C soil temperature**.
  Managed by **resistant varieties, long rotation, mixed cropping with sorghum (which is documented
  to reduce pigeonpea wilt), *Trichoderma* seed treatment and soil application, and organic
  amendment**.

### *Fusarium* grain moulds and mycotoxins — a food-safety dimension
- ***F. verticillioides*** in maize produces **fumonisins**; ***F. graminearum*** in wheat produces
  **deoxynivalenol (DON, "vomitoxin")** and **zearalenone**. **Fusarium head blight of wheat** is
  favoured by **warm, wet weather (25–30 °C with prolonged wetness) exactly at flowering**, which is
  the infection window; bleached, prematurely whitened spikelets with **pink-salmon spore masses**
  at the glume base are the sign.
- The practical message is one of **grain handling as much as field management**: **harvest at the
  correct moisture, dry grain promptly to a safe moisture content, avoid damaged and insect-bored
  grain, clean and grade to remove shrivelled kernels, and store dry** — because mycotoxins
  accumulate in damp grain in store as well as in the field. **Insect damage in store opens the way
  for mould, so stored-grain insect management is also mycotoxin management.**

## B7. *Alternaria* — early blight, leaf blight, leaf spot, purple blotch

### What kind of organism, and what that means
***Alternaria* is a TRUE FUNGUS** (Ascomycota, Pleosporaceae), a **necrotroph** — it kills host
tissue ahead of itself with toxins and enzymes and then feeds on the dead tissue. Its conidia are
distinctive and easy to recognise: **obclavate (club-shaped, tapering to a beak), light to dark
brown, with BOTH transverse and longitudinal septa (muriform), and a prominent beak** — for
*A. macrospora* on cotton, **3–9 transverse septa and four longitudinal septa**, borne singly at
the apex of a **dark brown, 1–8 septate, irregularly bent conidiophore**.
(eagri PATH 272 Lecture 13 — http://eagri.org/eagri50/PATH272/lecture13/007.html)

**Fungicides act on fungi**, so *Alternaria* is addressed by true-fungus mode-of-action groups —
**FRAC 3 (DMI)**, **FRAC 7 (SDHI)**, **FRAC 9 (anilinopyrimidines)**, **FRAC 11 (QoI)**, and the
multi-site protectants **FRAC M03 (dithiocarbamates)** and **M05**. *Alternaria* is a **high
resistance-risk** genus — it is polycyclic, produces enormous numbers of conidia, and has developed
resistance to **FRAC 11** (via the G143A mutation and the F129L mutation) and to **FRAC 7** in
several crops worldwide, so **rotation of mode-of-action groups and partnering with a multi-site
protectant is essential**.

### The universal *Alternaria* symptom — concentric rings
**Each spot has a central lesion surrounded by CONCENTRIC RINGS**, giving the classic **"target
board" or "bull's eye" appearance**. This arises because the fungus grows in daily pulses driven by
the day/night cycle, laying down a ring of tissue death each cycle. **Concentric rings in a
brown leaf spot are the single most reliable field indicator of *Alternaria*.**
(eagri PATH 272 Lecture 13 — http://eagri.org/eagri50/PATH272/lecture13/007.html)

A second general feature: ***Alternaria* attacks OLDER, LOWER leaves FIRST** and moves upward,
because senescing tissue is more readily colonised by a necrotroph. **A disease that starts on the
lower leaves and moves up, with target-ringed brown spots, in warm humid weather, on a crop under
stress or short of nitrogen, is almost always *Alternaria*.**

### Key species in India
| Species | Disease | Crop |
|---|---|---|
| *A. solani* | **Early blight** | Potato, tomato, brinjal |
| *A. macrospora*, *A. gossypii* | Leaf blight | Cotton |
| *A. brassicae*, *A. brassicicola*, *A. raphani* | Leaf spot / blight | Mustard, rapeseed, cabbage, cauliflower |
| *A. porri* | **Purple blotch** | Onion, garlic |
| *A. helianthi* | Leaf blight | Sunflower |
| *A. alternata* | Leaf spot, fruit rot, black point | Very wide host range |
| *A. sesami* | Leaf blight | Sesame |
| *A. carthami* | Leaf spot | Safflower |
| *A. triticina* | Leaf blight | Wheat |

### Cotton leaf blight — *A. macrospora* (reference case)
**Symptoms.** The disease may occur at all stages but is **more severe when plants are 45–60 days
old**. **Small, pale to brown, irregular or round spots, 0.5–6 mm diameter**, appear on the leaves.
**Each spot has a central lesion surrounded by concentric rings.** Several spots coalesce to form
blighted areas; **affected leaves become brittle and fall off**. Stem lesions occur, and in severe
cases spots appear on bracts and bolls.

**Favourable conditions.** **High humidity; intermittent rains; moderate temperature of 25–28 °C.**

**Disease cycle.** *"The pathogen survives in the dead leaves as dormant mycelium. The pathogen
primarily spreads through irrigation water. The secondary spread is mainly by airborne conidia."*
(eagri PATH 272 Lecture 13 — http://eagri.org/eagri50/PATH272/lecture13/007.html)

**Management.** **Remove and destroy infected plant residues**; treat at the first appearance of
disease, with repeat applications at about 15-day intervals, rotating mode-of-action groups.

### Early blight — *Alternaria solani* on potato and tomato
- **Symptoms**: dark brown to black spots on the **older, lower leaves first**, with pronounced
  **concentric rings** and often a **yellow halo**. Lesions on stems are elongated and ringed. On
  tomato fruit, **dark, leathery, sunken lesions at the stem end (calyx end)**, often with a velvety
  black spore layer. On potato tubers, **dark, sunken, corky lesions with a raised margin**. Severe
  defoliation exposes tubers to sun and reduces bulking.
- **A key stress relationship**: *A. solani* is strongly favoured by **plants under stress and short
  of nitrogen**, by **older plants approaching maturity**, and by **alternating wet and dry
  periods**. **Maintaining adequate, balanced nutrition through the season directly reduces early
  blight** — a nutritional control for a fungal disease.
- **Favourable weather**: **temperature 24–29 °C** with **relative humidity above 90 %** and
  **leaf wetness of 2 or more hours**; alternating wet and dry cycles are more favourable than
  continuous wetness. ⚠
- **Survival**: **as mycelium and conidia in infected plant debris in and on the soil, in volunteer
  potato and tomato plants, in infected tubers, on solanaceous weeds (*Solanum nigrum*, *Datura*,
  *Physalis*), and on and in seed.** Survives in debris for **at least one season, commonly cited as
  up to 1–2 years**. ⚠
- **Distinguishing early blight from late blight** — a very high-value discrimination because they
  are different kingdoms and different chemistry:

| | **Early blight (*Alternaria solani*, TRUE FUNGUS)** | **Late blight (*Phytophthora infestans*, OOMYCETE)** |
|---|---|---|
| Weather | **Warm 24–29 °C**, humid, alternating wet/dry | **Cool 10–22 °C**, continuously wet, foggy, cloudy |
| Lesion | **Dry, brown, firm, with CONCENTRIC RINGS**, bounded and discrete | **Water-soaked, greasy, spreading, irregular, no rings**, often starting at leaf tips/margins |
| White growth | Absent | **White downy sporulating ring on the LOWER leaf surface at the lesion margin in the morning** |
| Starts on | **Older lower leaves** | **Any leaf**, often upper and outer, spreading fast |
| Speed | Progressive over weeks | **A field can collapse in 3–7 days** |
| Smell | None | A distinctive musty odour in severe attacks |
| Chemistry | True-fungus groups (FRAC 3, 7, 9, 11, M03) | **Oomycete groups (FRAC 4, 21, 40, 43, 45, 49) plus multi-site protectants** |

### Purple blotch of onion — *Alternaria porri*
**Small white sunken flecks with purple centres**, enlarging into **elongated, zonate (concentrically
ringed) purple-brown lesions with a yellow margin**, usually **starting on older leaves**; the leaf
**breaks over ("lodges") at the lesion**, which is the practical loss because the bulb stops
filling. Also causes **neck rot in storage**. Favoured by **temperature 21–30 °C with relative
humidity above 80–90 % and leaf wetness**, and by the **monsoon and post-monsoon in the Indian
kharif onion crop**. Survives in **onion debris, in cull piles, on volunteer onions and on
onion/garlic seed and bulbs**. **Thrips injury greatly increases purple blotch**, because the
rasping wounds provide entry — so **thrips management is purple blotch management**. Managed by
**crop rotation of 2–3 years out of alliums, wider spacing and good drainage, removal of debris and
cull piles, clean seed and bulbs, adequate potassium, thrips management, curing bulbs properly
before storage, and protectant fungicide programmes rotated by FRAC group**. ⚠

### *Alternaria* blight of mustard and rapeseed — *A. brassicae* / *A. brassicicola*
The most important disease of Indian mustard. **Greyish-brown to black round spots with prominent
concentric rings on leaves**, spreading to stem and pods; **pod infection reduces seed size and oil
content** and is the main yield loss. Favoured by **temperature 20–27 °C with relative humidity
above 90 %, and by rain or heavy dew at the pod formation stage**. **Both seed-borne and
debris-borne**; survives in **crop residue and on cruciferous weeds**. Managed by **timely (early)
sowing so that pod formation escapes the humid late-season window, clean seed and seed treatment,
crop residue destruction, balanced nutrition with adequate potassium and sulphur, wider spacing,
removal of cruciferous weeds, and protectant applications timed to the first appearance of spots on
the lower leaves and repeated at the pod stage**. ⚠

## B8. *Colletotrichum* — anthracnose, dieback, fruit rot, red rot

### What kind of organism, and what that means
***Colletotrichum* is a TRUE FUNGUS** (Ascomycota, Glomerellaceae; sexual state *Glomerella*). It
produces its spores in **acervuli** — saucer-shaped fruiting structures that break through the host
epidermis — and characteristically bears **numerous black, thick-walled SETAE (stiff bristles)**
among the spores in the acervulus. **Conidia are hyaline and either falcate (sickle-shaped, as in
*C. capsici* and *C. truncatum*) or cylindrical with rounded ends (as in *C. gloeosporioides*)**.
(eagri PATH 272 Lecture 13 — http://eagri.org/eagri50/PATH272/lecture13/004.html)

**In the field, the signature of anthracnose is a sunken, dark lesion with concentric rings of
minute black dots (the acervuli), which in humid weather exude a PINK, SALMON or ORANGE slimy spore
mass.** That pink spore ooze is the diagnosis. It also tells you how the fungus moves: **the spores
are held in a water-soluble, sticky mucilage, so they are dispersed by RAIN SPLASH and by
irrigation droplets, not by wind.** Consequently:
- **Anthracnose is a rain-splash disease.** It moves upward and outward from a focus, plant to
  plant, in splashing rain and overhead irrigation.
- **Overhead irrigation spreads it; drip or furrow irrigation does not.**
- **Mulching between rows blocks the splash of soil-borne inoculum onto lower leaves and fruit.**
- **Working in a wet crop moves spores on hands, clothing and tools.**

**Fungicides act on fungi**, so *Colletotrichum* is addressed by true-fungus groups — **FRAC 1
(benzimidazoles)**, **FRAC 3 (DMI)**, **FRAC 7 (SDHI)**, **FRAC 11 (QoI)**, and multi-site
protectants **FRAC M01 (copper)** and **M03 (dithiocarbamates)**. ⚠ **FRAC 1 resistance is
widespread in *Colletotrichum* worldwide**, and **FRAC 11 resistance is documented**, so mode-of-
action rotation matters especially here.

### The quiescent (latent) infection — why fruit rots in transit
This is the most important single concept for *Colletotrichum* on fruit crops such as **mango,
banana, papaya, chilli, guava and grape**. The fungus **infects the young green fruit in the field
during the rainy season, then stops growing** — the appressorium sits dormant beneath the cuticle,
held in check by antifungal compounds in the unripe fruit. **The infection resumes only when the
fruit ripens** and those compounds decline.

The consequences are large and non-obvious to a farmer:
- **A fruit can look perfectly healthy at harvest and rot in the box three days later.**
- **The infection happened weeks or months earlier, in the field, in the rain.** Therefore
  **anthracnose of mango is controlled by sprays timed to flowering and early fruit development in
  the rainy period, not by anything done after harvest.**
- **Post-harvest hot-water treatment (commonly about 50–52 °C for 5–10 minutes for mango) kills the
  quiescent infection under the skin** — a physical treatment that reaches where a surface spray
  cannot. ⚠ Exact temperature and time differ by fruit and cultivar and must be followed precisely,
  because the margin between effective and injurious is narrow.

### Key species and diseases in India
| Species | Disease | Crop |
|---|---|---|
| *C. capsici* / *C. truncatum* | **Anthracnose / "die-back and fruit rot"**, ripe fruit rot | Chilli, soybean, cotton |
| *C. gloeosporioides* (*Glomerella cingulata*) | **Anthracnose, blossom blight, dieback, post-harvest rot** | Mango, banana, papaya, guava, citrus, grape, cashew, rubber |
| *C. lindemuthianum* | Bean anthracnose | French bean, common bean |
| *C. falcatum* | **Red rot** | Sugarcane |
| *C. musae* | Crown rot / anthracnose | Banana |
| *C. graminicola* | Anthracnose stalk rot | Maize, sorghum |
| *C. coccodes* | Black dot | Potato, tomato |

### Cotton anthracnose — *C. capsici* (reference case)
**Symptoms.** Infects seedlings, producing **small reddish circular spots on the cotyledons and
primary leaves**; **lesions on the collar region may girdle the stem, causing the seedling to wilt
and die**. In mature plants the fungus attacks the stem, causing **stem splitting and shredding of
the bark**. **The most common symptom is boll spotting** — **small, water-soaked, circular,
reddish-brown DEPRESSED (sunken) spots on the bolls**. The **lint is stained yellow or brown and
becomes a solid brittle mass of fibre**; infected bolls **stop growing, burst and dry up
prematurely**.
(eagri PATH 272 Lecture 13 — http://eagri.org/eagri50/PATH272/lecture13/004.html)

**Favourable conditions.** **Prolonged rainfall at the time of boll formation; close planting.**

**Disease cycle and survival.**
*"The pathogen survives as dormant mycelium in the seed or as conidia on the surface of seeds for
about a year. The pathogen also perpetuates on the rotten bolls and other plant debris in the soil.
The secondary spread is by air-borne conidia. The pathogen also survives in the weed hosts viz.,
*Aristolochia bracteata* and *Hibiscus diversifolius*."*
(eagri PATH 272 Lecture 13 — http://eagri.org/eagri50/PATH272/lecture13/004.html)

**Both seed-borne (internally as dormant mycelium and externally as conidia, surviving about
1 year) and debris-borne.**

**Management (positively stated).** **Treat delinted seed**; **remove and burn infected plant debris
and bolls from the soil**; **rogue out the named weed hosts**; **adopt wider spacing so the canopy
dries**; **apply protectants at the boll formation stage**, timed to the rain forecast.

### Chilli anthracnose / dieback — *C. capsici* and *C. truncatum*
The most important chilli disease in India after the viruses. Two symptom phases from one pathogen:
- **Dieback**: infection of the **twigs and flowers**, causing **blossom blight** and **progressive
  drying of the twig from the tip downward**, with the plant becoming stunted and bare.
- **Ripe fruit rot**: **sunken, circular, water-soaked lesions on the fruit with concentric rings of
  black acervuli**, exuding **pink spore masses** in humid weather. **Red (ripe) fruit is far more
  susceptible than green fruit**, which is the quiescent-infection effect again — infection occurs
  on the green fruit and expresses at ripening. Seed from infected fruit carries the fungus, so
  **saving seed from an affected crop propagates the disease**.
Favoured by **temperature 25–30 °C with relative humidity above 90 % and rainfall during the fruit
maturation period**. Managed by **certified or treated seed, wider spacing, staking to keep fruit
off the ground, mulching to stop splash, harvesting ripe fruit promptly rather than letting it hang,
removing and destroying infected fruit and twigs, drying harvested chilli quickly on clean surfaces
rather than on bare soil, rotation, and protectant programmes rotated by FRAC group and timed to
flowering and fruit set in rainy periods**. ⚠

### Mango anthracnose — *C. gloeosporioides*
**Black, sunken, irregular spots on leaves, blossom blight that blackens and kills the panicle
(a major cause of poor fruit set), "tear-stain" streaks running down the fruit where spore-laden
water has trickled, and post-harvest fruit rot from quiescent infections.** The fungus survives on
**mummified fruit, dead twigs, old panicles and fallen leaves in the orchard**, and also
**endophytically in symptomless twigs**. Spread by **rain splash and by wind-driven rain**. It is
**favoured by 25–30 °C with prolonged wetness and by rain during flowering**, which is why
**unseasonal rain at flowering is so damaging to mango**. Managed by **orchard sanitation removing
mummified fruit, dead twigs and fallen leaves; pruning to open the canopy for air movement and
light; avoiding overhead irrigation; protectant sprays timed to panicle emergence, flowering and
early fruit set; and post-harvest hot-water treatment plus prompt cooling**. ⚠

### Red rot of sugarcane — *C. falcatum*
The historically most destructive sugarcane disease in India. **The diagnosis is made by splitting
the cane lengthwise**: the internal tissue shows **reddened pith interrupted by TRANSVERSE WHITE
PATCHES ACROSS the reddened area** — the white cross-bands are the definitive character — together
with an **alcoholic, sour smell**. Externally, the **third or fourth leaf yellows and dries**, the
cane withers, and **small dark red spots with acervuli appear on the midrib**.
- **Primary spread is through infected setts** — again the vegetatively propagated crop problem.
- **Secondary spread is by rain splash, irrigation water and wind-blown conidia**, entering through
  **wounds, growth cracks, borer holes and leaf scars**. **Borer damage predisposes cane to red
  rot**, so **borer management is red rot management**.
- Survives in **diseased setts, in stubble, in crop debris and in soil for several months**.
- **Varietal resistance breaks down repeatedly** because *C. falcatum* has many **pathotypes (CF01,
  CF07, CF08, CF09 and others)**, and Indian sugarcane history is a sequence of popular varieties
  (Co 419, CoC 671, Co 7717, CoJ 64, Co 0238) becoming susceptible after a decade of wide planting.
  **Co 0238**, which dominated the Uttar Pradesh belt, succumbed to red rot from around 2020 — the
  boom-and-bust cycle in a single, well-documented Indian example.
- Managed by **planting only healthy setts from a disease-free three-tier seed nursery, hot-water
  or moist-hot-air treatment of setts, sett treatment with fungicide or *Trichoderma*, roguing and
  removal of affected clumps with the surrounding soil, avoiding ratooning of a diseased crop,
  crop rotation, good drainage (waterlogging strongly aggravates red rot), borer management, and
  periodic varietal replacement following ICAR-IISR/state recommendations**. ⚠

## B9. Begomoviruses — leaf curl, yellow mosaic, yellow vein mosaic

### What kind of organism, and what that means
**Begomoviruses are VIRUSES** — genus *Begomovirus*, family **Geminiviridae**. The particle is a
**geminate (twinned) icosahedron**, and the genome is **circular single-stranded DNA**, either
**bipartite (DNA-A and DNA-B)** or **monopartite**, very often accompanied by a small satellite
molecule called a **betasatellite (DNA-β)** and by **alphasatellites**.
(eagri PATH 272 Lecture 13 — http://eagri.org/eagri50/PATH272/lecture13/009.html)

**Because it is a virus, it multiplies only inside living plant cells and there is nothing on the
plant surface for a spray to act on. Begomovirus diseases are therefore managed by: managing the
whitefly vector, planting clean and certified planting material, roguing infected plants early,
removing weed and volunteer hosts, using barrier and border crops and protected nurseries, adjusting
the planting date to avoid the vector peak, and growing resistant or tolerant varieties.**

### The vector — one insect, the entire group
**Every begomovirus is transmitted by the whitefly *Bemisia tabaci*** (see the entomology file,
Section 1). Transmission is **persistent and circulative**:
- **Acquisition** from an infected plant takes **15–30 minutes** of feeding.
- The virus passes through the gut wall into the haemolymph and to the salivary glands over a
  **latent period of about 6–8 hours**.
- The whitefly then **remains infective for the rest of its life** (weeks).
- Transmission is **circulative but generally NOT propagative** — the virus does not multiply in
  the insect ⚠ (evidence for limited replication in the vector exists for some begomoviruses).

**Why this matters for advice.** Because the vector stays infective for life, **even a small number
of whiteflies can produce a high incidence of virus**. The **whitefly threshold for direct feeding
damage (5–10 nymphs per leaf in cotton) is far above the level at which virus spread is
significant.** So in a virus-prone crop the decision rule changes: **act on the whitefly to protect
against the virus long before the numbers reach the feeding-damage threshold, and concentrate
everything on the first 30–60 days of crop life, when infection causes the greatest yield loss.**

**Equally important**: because the virus is **not transmitted by seed, by sap or by contact**, a
farmer can **handle and prune an infected plant without spreading the virus** — the risk is entirely
through the insect. *"Not transmitted by seed or contact."*
(eagri PATH 272 Lecture 13 — http://eagri.org/eagri50/PATH272/lecture13/009.html)
⚠ Exceptions exist: a few begomoviruses (notably some tomato and squash leaf curl species) have been
reported as seed-transmissible at low rates, and this is an area of active research. Vegetatively
propagated crops (cassava, sweet potato) do carry begomoviruses in the planting material.

### Major begomovirus diseases in India
| Disease | Virus | Crop |
|---|---|---|
| **Cotton leaf curl disease (CLCuD)** | Cotton leaf curl Multan/Kokhran/Rajasthan/Burewala viruses + **Cotton leaf curl Multan betasatellite** | Cotton (north-west India) |
| **Tomato leaf curl** | Tomato leaf curl New Delhi virus (ToLCNDV), ToLCBaV and others | Tomato, cucurbits |
| **Chilli leaf curl** | Chilli leaf curl virus (ChiLCV) | Chilli |
| **Yellow mosaic of pulses** | Mungbean yellow mosaic virus (MYMV), Mungbean yellow mosaic India virus (MYMIV) | Mungbean, urdbean, soybean, French bean |
| **Bhendi/okra yellow vein mosaic** | Bhendi yellow vein mosaic virus (BYVMV) + betasatellite | Okra |
| **Okra enation leaf curl** | Okra enation leaf curl virus | Okra |
| **Cassava mosaic** | Indian cassava mosaic virus, Sri Lankan cassava mosaic virus | Cassava |
| **Tomato yellow leaf curl** | TYLCV | Tomato |
| **Papaya leaf curl** | Papaya leaf curl virus | Papaya |
| **Croton yellow vein mosaic, Ageratum enation** | — | Weeds acting as reservoirs |

### Cotton leaf curl disease — the reference case
**Symptoms.** *"Downward and upward curling of leaves and thickening of veins and ENATION on the
underside of leaves are the characteristic symptoms. In severe infection all the leaves are curled
and growth is retarded. Boll bearing capacity is reduced."*
(eagri PATH 272 Lecture 13 — http://eagri.org/eagri50/PATH272/lecture13/009.html)

**The definitive diagnostic is the ENATION** — a small **leaf-like or cup-shaped outgrowth on the
underside of the leaf, arising from a thickened vein**. Nothing else in cotton produces enations.
Together with **thickened, darkened veins visible when the leaf is held up to the light (vein
thickening / vein clearing)**, this separates CLCuD from **jassid hopper burn** (downward margin
curl with reddening but no vein thickening and no enation), from **whitefly feeding damage** (sooty
mould, no distortion of veins), and from **herbicide drift** (strap-shaped leaves, no enations).

**Disease cycle.** *"The primary source is the viruliferous whitefly vector *Bemisia tabaci*. The
alternate hosts and cultivated hosts serve as virus reservoirs throughout the year."*
(eagri PATH 272 Lecture 13 — http://eagri.org/eagri50/PATH272/lecture13/009.html)

**Reservoir hosts** — the year-round survival is entirely in living plants:
- **Cultivated**: okra, tomato, chilli, brinjal, tobacco, papaya, and **volunteer/perennial cotton**.
- **Weeds**: ***Hibiscus* spp., wild okra (*Abelmoschus* spp.), *Abutilon indicum*, *Sida* spp.,
  *Croton bonplandianum*, *Ageratum conyzoides*, *Xanthium strumarium*, *Digera arvensis*,
  *Malvastrum coromandelianum*.** ⚠

**Management (positively stated), from the TNAU package.**
- **Manage the planting date to avoid the peak vector population** — sowing so the vulnerable
  first 60 days fall outside the whitefly peak is one of the most effective actions available.
- **Eliminate volunteer and perennial cotton and alternate hosts, including malvaceous hosts like
  wild okra.**
- **Use the fungus *Paecilomyces farinosus*, which parasitises *B. tabaci* and brings down the
  vector population.**
- **Apply neem leaf extract and 1 % neem oil** — recorded as giving an **80 % reduction in virus
  transmission**, because neem acts as a feeding deterrent and reduces the probing that transmits
  the virus.
- **Manage the vector**, concentrating on the early crop.
(eagri PATH 272 Lecture 13 — http://eagri.org/eagri50/PATH272/lecture13/009.html)

Additional measures that follow from the biology:
- **Grow resistant/tolerant varieties and hybrids** as the primary line. In north-west India,
  CLCuD-tolerant material carrying resistance from *Gossypium arboreum* and from screened
  *G. hirsutum* lines is central. **Desi cotton (*G. arboreum*) is notably tolerant to CLCuD**, which
  is why it is recommended in high-pressure districts.
- **Rogue and remove infected plants early**, especially in the first 30–45 days, so they do not
  become sources.
- **Raise nurseries under 40–50 mesh insect-proof net** for transplanted crops.
- **Use border/barrier rows of tall non-host crops (maize, sorghum, bajra)** — the whitefly is a weak
  flier and a physical barrier reduces immigration.
- **Use reflective/silver-grey plastic mulch**, which disorients incoming whiteflies and measurably
  reduces early infection.
- **Maintain a host-free period** and **synchronise sowing across a village**, both of which break
  the continuous green bridge the vector and virus need.
- **Apply nitrogen judiciously** — lush growth attracts whitefly and increases infection.

### Yellow mosaic of pulses — MYMV / MYMIV
**Symptoms**: **bright yellow, irregular scattered patches on green leaves**, progressing to complete
yellowing; leaves become smaller; the plant is stunted; **pods are few, small, distorted, and turn
yellow**, and seed is shrivelled. In mungbean and urdbean the loss can be total if infection is
early. **Infection in the first 3–4 weeks after sowing causes far greater loss than later
infection**, which is why the early-crop period is the whole battle.
**Management**: **resistant/tolerant varieties are the mainstay and India has good MYMV-resistant
mungbean and urdbean varieties** (a rare case where varietal resistance carries most of the load);
**sow at the recommended time to escape the whitefly peak**; **rogue infected plants in the first
month**; **remove weed hosts**; **avoid growing susceptible pulses adjacent to cotton, soybean or
other whitefly-rich crops**; **use intercropping with cereals**; **manage the whitefly early**.

### Okra yellow vein mosaic — BYVMV
**Symptoms**: **the VEINS turn yellow while the interveinal tissue stays green**, producing a
striking **yellow network**; in severe cases the whole leaf yellows, and **fruits become pale
yellow, small, hard and unmarketable**. Because okra is picked continuously and infection reduces
every subsequent pick, **early roguing is highly cost-effective**. The virus requires a
**betasatellite** to produce full symptoms — a useful teaching point about why the same virus can be
mild in one place and severe in another.

### Why begomovirus resistance breaks down
1. **Recombination.** Begomoviruses recombine readily when two species infect the same plant. The
   **Burewala strain** of cotton leaf curl, which broke the resistance deployed after the 1990s
   epidemic in the north-west, arose by recombination. Mixed infections in a weed or crop reservoir
   are the reactor in which new variants are made — which is another reason weed host removal
   matters beyond simple inoculum reduction.
2. **Betasatellites.** A monopartite begomovirus plus a **betasatellite** produces much more severe
   symptoms and can overcome tolerance. Betasatellites are **exchanged between viruses**, so a
   locally mild virus can acquire a severe partner.
3. **Very high mutation rate.** Despite a DNA genome, begomoviruses mutate at rates comparable to
   RNA viruses.
4. **Uniform deployment.** A single tolerant variety planted across a whole region applies uniform
   selection.
5. **Vector shifts.** Displacement of one *B. tabaci* cryptic species by another (for example the
   spread of the Asia-II-1 and MEAM1 groups) changes which virus is efficiently transmitted and can
   make a previously minor virus dominant.

**What makes it durable**: **combining several tolerance sources, deploying a diversity of varieties
across a landscape, keeping the vector population low so fewer inoculations occur and fewer mixed
infections form, and removing the weed reservoirs where recombination happens.**
