# Entomology Reference — Insect and Mite Pests of Indian Crops

Purpose: field-usable biology for a farmer advisory device. Each entry covers life cycle with
stage durations in days, where each stage physically sits on the plant or in the soil, seasonal
peaks in India, off-season survival, alternate and weed hosts, spread, published economic
thresholds, named Indian natural enemies, and virus transmission.

Scope note: this file contains biology and ecology only. No pesticide names and no doses appear
here by design. Management statements are cultural, mechanical, and biological.

Reading convention: ⚠ marks a figure that is uncertain, varies widely between published sources,
or is region-specific.

## 1. Whitefly — *Bemisia tabaci* (Hemiptera: Aleyrodidae)

### What it is
A tiny sap-sucking hemipteran with a white waxy bloom over a yellow body. It is a *species complex*
of many cryptic species and biotypes; in India the Asia-I, Asia-II-1 and the invasive Mediterranean
(MEAM1/B and MED/Q) groups occur. Biotype identity matters because it changes host preference and
virus transmission efficiency. (eagri ENTO 331 Lecture 10 —
http://eagri.org/eagri50/ENTO331/lecture10/004.html)

### Life cycle and stage durations
- **Egg**: pear-shaped, yellowish-white, laid singly on the **under surface of leaves**, attached by
  a short stalk inserted into leaf tissue. Egg period **3 days** (TNAU/eagri figure); other Indian
  work reports **3–5 days** depending on temperature. ⚠ Range widens to 5–9 days in cool weather.
- **Nymph (crawler + 3 sessile instars)**: the first instar "crawler" walks a short distance, then
  settles permanently. Instars 2 and 3 are flat, oval, scale-like and immobile on the **lower leaf
  surface**. Nymphal period **5–33 days in summer** and **17–73 days in winter**.
  (eagri ENTO 331 Lecture 10 — http://eagri.org/eagri50/ENTO331/lecture10/004.html)
- **Puparium ("pupa", 4th instar)**: oval, on the **under surface of the leaf**, with red eyespots
  visible before adult emergence. This is the stage used for species identification.
- **Adult**: emerges through a T-shaped slit in the puparium. Adult longevity commonly
  **10–20 days**, females longer than males; fecundity in the range **80–300 eggs per female**. ⚠
- **Total generation**: about **14–21 days in hot weather**, extending past 60 days in winter.
  **11–15 overlapping generations per year** in central and southern India. ⚠ Generation count is
  location-specific and continuous where a green host is always available.

### Where to look — stage location
Turn leaves over. Adults sit and fly up from the **underside of young top leaves**; eggs and
nymphs are on the **underside of middle and lower leaves** because females lay on the youngest
leaf available and the leaf then ages beneath them. This vertical distribution is the basis of
scouting: count **adults on top leaves, nymphs on middle leaves**.

### Seasonal peak in India and weather that builds populations
- Build-up is favoured by **hot, dry weather**: temperature roughly **25–33 °C** with
  **moderate to low relative humidity**, and prolonged dry spells. ⚠
- Peak in north Indian cotton (Punjab, Haryana, Rajasthan) is typically **August to September**,
  after the monsoon break; the 2015 Punjab whitefly outbreak followed exactly this pattern.
- In central and south India peaks fall in **September to November** and again in the
  **February to April** dry season on vegetables.
- **Heavy, continuous rainfall physically dislodges nymphs and adults and suppresses populations**;
  a monsoon break followed by heat is the classic outbreak trigger.
- Populations rise where nitrogen is applied heavily and where irrigation keeps plants lush and
  soft. Balanced nitrogen and judicious irrigation keep populations lower.
  (eagri ENTO 331 Lecture 10; ICAR-CICR advisories — https://cicr.org.in/)

### Off-season survival (the carry-over bridge)
*B. tabaci* has **no true diapause**. It survives entirely by moving onto **living green hosts**:
- Volunteer and ratoon cotton, and cotton stubble left standing after harvest.
- Winter vegetable crops — brinjal, tomato, okra, cucurbits, cole crops.
- Perennial weeds on bunds, canal banks and wasteland.
This makes a **host-free window** the single most powerful control: removing and destroying cotton
stalks immediately after the last pick, avoiding ratoon and a second cotton crop in the same year,
and destroying volunteer plants break the bridge between seasons.
(eagri ENTO 331 Lecture 10 — http://eagri.org/eagri50/ENTO331/lecture10/004.html)

### Host range — cultivated
Cotton, tomato, tobacco, sweet potato, cassava, cabbage, cauliflower, melon, brinjal and okra
(bhendi); also chilli, soybean, mungbean, urdbean, and cucurbits generally. Over 600 plant
species worldwide. (eagri ENTO 331 Lecture 10)

### Alternate and weed hosts — highest-value cultural action
Named weed hosts to remove from field bunds, channels and adjoining wasteland:
- *Abutilon indicum* (country mallow, kanghi) — a malvaceous weed and a reservoir for both the
  whitefly and cotton leaf curl virus.
- *Solanum nigrum* (black nightshade, makoi)
- *Croton sparsiflorus*, *Xanthium strumarium* (cocklebur), *Parthenium hysterophorus* (congress
  grass), *Sida* spp., *Digera arvensis*, *Euphorbia* spp., *Acalypha indica*, *Convolvulus
  arvensis*. ⚠ Weed reservoir lists vary by state; *Abutilon* and *Solanum nigrum* are the two
  named explicitly in the TNAU/eagri package.
(eagri ENTO 331 Lecture 10 — http://eagri.org/eagri50/ENTO331/lecture10/004.html)

### How it spreads
- **Adult flight**: weak fliers on their own, moving a few metres between plants, but they are
  **carried long distances on wind currents** — tens of kilometres in a single wind event. This is
  why area-wide, village-level synchrony matters more than single-field action.
- **Planting material and nursery seedlings**: eggs and nymphs travel on the underside of leaves of
  transplanted vegetable seedlings. Raising nursery under insect-proof net (40–50 mesh) delivers
  clean seedlings.
- **Transport of produce and plant waste** moves all immature stages.
- **Machinery, clothing and workers** move adults short distances within a farm.

### Economic Threshold Level (ETL)
- **Cotton: 5–10 nymphs per leaf** (eagri ENTO 331 Lecture 10 —
  http://eagri.org/eagri50/ENTO331/lecture10/004.html)
- A widely used field alternative in north India: **6–8 adults per leaf** on the top 3 leaves,
  averaged across 20 plants. ⚠ Published thresholds differ between state packages; treat the
  TNAU 5–10 nymphs/leaf figure as the citable one.
- Monitoring aid: **yellow sticky traps and yellow pan traps set at about 1 foot (30 cm) above the
  canopy**, raised as the crop grows. Yellow is attractive to adults, so traps give an early warning
  of immigration. (eagri ENTO 331 Lecture 10)

### Natural enemies in India — named
- **Parasitoids (Aphelinidae)**: *Encarsia* spp. — *Encarsia formosa*, *E. sophia* (= *E.
  transvena*), *E. guadeloupae*; and *Eretmocerus* spp. — *Eretmocerus mundus*, *E. corni*. These
  are tiny wasps whose larvae develop inside whitefly nymphs; a parasitised nymph turns dark
  brown or black and is easy to see on the leaf underside. Seeing black nymphs is a sign to hold
  back on sprays.
- **Predators**: *Chrysoperla zastrowi sillemi* (green lacewing), coccinellids *Serangium
  parcesetosum*, *Cheilomenes* (= *Menochilus*) *sexmaculata*, *Coccinella septempunctata*; the
  predatory mite *Amblyseius swirskii*; predatory bugs *Orius* spp. and *Geocoris* spp.; spiders.
- **Entomopathogenic fungi**: *Lecanicillium lecanii* (= *Verticillium lecanii*), *Beauveria
  bassiana*, *Isaria fumosorosea*. These act best under **high humidity** and are favoured by the
  same moist conditions that suppress whitefly directly.
- **Conservation**: grow pulse intercrops and border rows (cowpea, greengram, blackgram, soybean)
  which support predator populations; keep flowering bunds for nectar; use selective, need-based
  intervention driven by ETL counts; minimise broad-spectrum synthetic pyrethroid use, which is
  explicitly flagged as causing whitefly resurgence.
  (eagri ENTO 331 Lecture 10 and Lecture 11 — http://eagri.org/eagri50/ENTO331/lecture11/002.html;
  ICAR-NBAIR — https://www.nbair.res.in/)

### Virus transmission — this is the main economic damage
*B. tabaci* is the **sole field vector of the genus *Begomovirus*** (family Geminiviridae) and
transmits several other virus genera. Transmission of begomoviruses is **persistent and
circulative**: the virus is acquired in 15–30 minutes of feeding, passes through the gut into the
haemolymph and salivary glands over a **latent period of 6–8 hours**, and the insect then remains
infective for the rest of its life. Because retention is lifelong, **even low whitefly numbers can
cause high virus incidence** — the threshold for virus management is far below the threshold for
direct feeding damage.

Viruses transmitted in India:
- **Cotton leaf curl virus (CLCuV / CLCuD complex)** — cotton, north India
- **Tomato leaf curl virus (ToLCV)** — tomato
- **Chilli leaf curl virus (ChiLCV)** — chilli
- **Okra yellow vein mosaic virus (OYVMV)** and **Bhendi yellow vein mosaic**
- **Mungbean yellow mosaic virus / Mungbean yellow mosaic India virus (MYMV / MYMIV)** — mungbean,
  urdbean, soybean
- **Tomato yellow leaf curl virus (TYLCV)**
- **Indian cassava mosaic virus (ICMV)**
- Also **Criniviruses** (e.g. Tomato chlorosis virus) and **Carlaviruses** (Cowpea mild mottle
  virus) — semipersistent or non-persistent.
(eagri ENTO 331 Lecture 10; ICAR-CICR — https://cicr.org.in/)

### Farmer-facing actions supported by this biology
1. Destroy cotton stalks and volunteer plants at once after final pick — removes the green bridge.
2. Grow cotton once a year only; avoid ratoon and a second overlapping crop.
3. Rotate with non-preferred hosts — sorghum, ragi, maize, other cereals.
4. Remove *Abutilon indicum* and *Solanum nigrum* from bunds and neighbouring waste ground.
5. Keep brinjal, okra, tomato and tobacco away from the cotton field boundary.
6. Sow on time and at recommended (preferably wider) spacing; late sowing carries higher risk.
7. Apply nitrogen judiciously and irrigate judiciously to avoid lush, soft growth.
8. Set yellow sticky traps at canopy height to detect immigration early.
9. Collect and destroy infested and shed leaves.
10. Raise vegetable nurseries under 40–50 mesh insect-proof net.

## 2. Cotton leafhopper / jassid — *Amrasca biguttula biguttula* (= *Amrasca devastans*) (Hemiptera: Cicadellidae)

### Life cycle and stage durations
- **Egg**: laid **singly, inserted inside the leaf veins** on the underside — so eggs are protected
  inside plant tissue and are invisible on the surface. **Incubation period 4–11 days.**
- **Nymph**: light green, translucent, found **between the veins on the under surface of leaves**.
  Nymphs walk characteristically **sideways (diagonally)** when disturbed — a reliable field
  identification cue. **Nymphal period 7–21 days**, with **five moults**.
- **Adult**: green, wedge-shaped, with a black spot on each forewing tip (hence *biguttula*,
  "two-spotted"). Adults jump and fly readily.
- **Total life cycle: 15–46 days. Eleven generations per year.**
(eagri ENTO 331 Lecture 10 — http://eagri.org/eagri50/ENTO331/lecture10/001.html)

### Where to look
Underside of leaves, in the **middle canopy**. Damage shows first at leaf margins.

### Damage and diagnosis
Both nymphs and adults suck sap from the under surface of leaves and inject toxic saliva. Tender
leaves turn yellow, **leaf margins curl downwards** (a downward cupping, distinct from the upward
cupping of thrips), then redden. Severe infestation gives a bronze or brick-red leaf — the classic
**"hopper burn"** — and growth is retarded.
(eagri ENTO 331 Lecture 10 — http://eagri.org/eagri50/ENTO331/lecture10/001.html)

### ETL
**50 nymphs/adults per 50 leaves**, i.e. an average of **1 per leaf**; or yellowing and curling
from the middle to the upper portion of the plant in **25 % of plants** in the field.
(eagri ENTO 331 Lecture 10)

### Host range and alternate hosts
Cotton, potato, brinjal, castor, okra (bhendi), tomato, hollyhock and sunflower. Okra is a
particularly important bridging host between cotton seasons, as is *Abutilon indicum*.

### Seasonal peak and weather
Peaks in the **rainy and immediately post-rainy period**, favoured by **moderate temperature with
high humidity**; populations are commonly highest **July to September**. Heavy driving rain
knocks nymphs off leaves. ⚠

### Off-season survival
Survives on okra, brinjal, castor, potato and malvaceous weeds through the non-cotton months.
There is no diapausing stage; survival depends on continuously available green hosts, so weed and
volunteer-host removal is the effective break.

### Spread
Strong adult flight and wind-assisted dispersal; immigration into a young crop from surrounding
okra and weed hosts is the usual source.

### Natural enemies in India
Green lacewing *Chrysoperla zastrowi sillemi* (cited in the TNAU package as *Chrysopa carnea*),
mymarid egg parasitoids *Anagrus* spp. and *Gonatocerus* spp. (they parasitise eggs inside the
vein), dryinid parasitoids, coccinellids, *Orius* spp., and spiders — especially lynx and jumping
spiders. Conserve by intercropping cotton with cowpea, greengram, blackgram or soybean, which
raises spider and coccinellid activity.
(eagri ENTO 331 Lecture 10 and Lecture 11 — http://eagri.org/eagri50/ENTO331/lecture11/003.html)

### Host-plant resistance
Leaf hairiness (pubescence) on the underside is the classical mechanism of jassid resistance in
cotton — hairy-leaved varieties carry far lower populations because the ovipositor and the nymphs
are physically obstructed. TNAU lists MCU 3, MCU 5 and MCU 9 as jassid-tolerant.
(eagri ENTO 331 Lecture 10)

### Virus transmission
No established plant virus is transmitted by *A. biguttula biguttula*. The damage is caused by
**toxicogenic feeding** — the saliva itself produces hopper burn.

## 3. Cotton thrips — *Thrips tabaci* (Thysanoptera: Thripidae)

### Damage and diagnosis
Both nymph and adult **rasp (lacerate) the leaf tissue and suck the exuding sap** from the upper
and lower leaf surfaces. Early attack shows a **silvery sheen on the lower surface**. With severe
infestation leaves **curl upward** and become crumpled — the upward curl separates thrips damage
from the downward curl of jassid damage.
(eagri ENTO 331 Lecture 10 — http://eagri.org/eagri50/ENTO331/lecture10/003.html)

### Bionomics
Adults are small, slender, yellowish to brown with **fringed (hair-edged) wings**; they drift away
on the slightest disturbance, so tapping a leaf over a white sheet or a dark surface is the
standard counting method. Nymphs are very minute, slender and yellowish.
Generalised stage durations for *Thrips tabaci*: **egg 4–5 days** (inserted into leaf tissue),
**two feeding larval instars 6–9 days**, then **prepupa and pupa 4–7 days spent mainly in the soil
or in leaf litter at the base of the plant**, adult longevity **2–3 weeks**. Total cycle about
**15–20 days at warm temperature**, giving many overlapping generations per year. ⚠ Stage
durations are not given in the TNAU cotton page; these are the standard published figures for the
species and should be treated as approximate.

### Where to look
Adults and larvae on the **youngest unfolding leaves and in the growing point**; the **pupal stage
is in the soil**, which is why soil surface disturbance and the condition of the top few
centimetres of soil influence populations.

### ETL
**1 thrips per leaf** (cotton). (eagri ENTO 331 Lecture 10 —
http://eagri.org/eagri50/ENTO331/lecture10/003.html)
Monitoring starts from the **14th day after sowing** for the early-season sucking pest complex.

### Seasonal peak and weather
Thrips build up under **hot, dry conditions with low relative humidity** and a break in rains;
overhead irrigation and rainfall suppress them mechanically. Peak in cotton is the
**early vegetative phase, 15–45 days after sowing**, and again in dry spells. In onion and chilli,
peaks fall in the **dry months of February to May**.

### Off-season survival and host range
Extremely polyphagous — onion (its classic host), garlic, cotton, cabbage, cauliflower, potato,
tobacco, tomato, cucurbits, and many weeds. It bridges seasons on **onion and garlic bulbs in
store, on volunteer alliums, and on weed hosts**; overlapping onion crops in a locality maintain
populations year-round. Some populations reproduce **thelytokously (parthenogenetically, all
female)**, so a single introduced female can start an infestation.

### Spread
Adults are weak fliers but are carried far on wind; they also travel on **planting material,
especially onion sets and bulbs, and on transplanted seedlings**.

### Natural enemies in India
Predatory thrips *Scolothrips indicus*, *Franklinothrips megalops* and *Aeolothrips* spp.;
anthocorid predatory bugs *Orius* spp.; predatory mites *Amblyseius* spp.; the entomopathogenic
fungi *Beauveria bassiana* and *Lecanicillium lecanii*, and the entomopathogenic nematode-like
agents in moist soil which reach the soil-dwelling pupal stage. Conserve by keeping flowering
border plants and by need-based intervention.
(eagri ENTO 331 Lecture 22 — http://eagri.org/eagri50/ENTO331/lecture22/chillies/001.html)

### Virus transmission — important
*Thrips tabaci* transmits **tospoviruses**, notably **Iris yellow spot virus (IYSV)** in onion —
a major cause of onion seed-crop loss in Maharashtra, Karnataka and Gujarat — and it is a vector
of **Tomato spotted wilt virus (TSWV)** in some populations. ⚠ TSWV transmission ability differs
strongly between *T. tabaci* populations; the onion-associated lineages are the efficient IYSV
vectors. Key biological point for advice: **only larvae that feed on an infected plant can acquire
a tospovirus; adults that acquire it as adults do not transmit.** Therefore controlling the crop
weeds and volunteers on which larvae develop is the effective lever against virus spread.

## 4. Chilli thrips — *Scirtothrips dorsalis* (Thysanoptera: Thripidae)

### Life cycle
Reproduces both **sexually and parthenogenetically** (arrhenotoky — unmated females produce males).
Females **insert eggs into leaf veins and into tender tissue** — eggs are therefore hidden inside
the plant and protected. A female lays **up to 40–48 eggs**. **Total life cycle 10–20 days.**
(eagri ENTO 331 Lecture 22 — http://eagri.org/eagri50/ENTO331/lecture22/chillies/001.html)

Breakdown of the cycle (standard published figures, to sit inside that 10–20 day total): **egg
4–6 days; two larval instars 6–8 days on the plant; prepupa 1 day and pupa 2–3 days, passed mainly
in the leaf axils, in flower bracts, in curled leaves and in the surface soil and litter**; adult
longevity about **13–20 days**. ⚠ These sub-stage figures are not in the TNAU page and vary with
temperature.
Many overlapping generations per year — effectively continuous where chilli is grown year-round.

### Where to look
On the **youngest terminal leaves and growing tips, and inside flowers**. The pupal stage in leaf
litter and surface soil is why removing crop debris matters.

### Damage and diagnosis — "murda" / leaf curl complex
Leaves become **crinkled and curl UPWARD (boat-shaped, cup-shaped upward)** and shed. Buds become
brittle and drop. Plants are stunted and bronzed. Nymphs and adults are tiny, slender, fragile and
yellowish-straw coloured.
(eagri ENTO 331 Lecture 22 — http://eagri.org/eagri50/ENTO331/lecture22/chillies/001.html)

**Critical diagnostic distinction for the advisory device:** in chilli, **upward curling with
bronzing = thrips (*S. dorsalis*)**; **downward curling with crinkling, blistering and elongated
"rat-tail" petioles = yellow/broad mite (*Polyphagotarsonemus latus*)**; **mixed, distorted, mosaic
and vein-clearing symptoms with stunting = chilli leaf curl begomovirus carried by whitefly**.
These three are routinely confused in the field and the correct call changes the entire
recommendation.

### Host range and alternate hosts
Tea, grapes, castor, cotton, *Prosopis* (mesquite/velikathan), *Nymphaea pubescens* (water lily);
also chilli, capsicum, mango, groundnut, rose, citrus, strawberry. Highly polyphagous — over 100
plant species.
(eagri ENTO 331 Lecture 22)

### Seasonal peak and weather
Peaks in **hot, dry weather** with a break in rains; in chilli the critical period is
**October to March** in the south and the **dry summer months** generally. Populations collapse in
prolonged heavy rain and under overhead sprinkler irrigation.

### Off-season survival
Survives on the wide perennial host range — mango, tea, castor, *Prosopis*, grapes and weeds —
and moves back onto the new chilli crop. There is no diapause.

### Cultural principles from TNAU
- Intercrop with **agathi (*Sesbania grandiflora*)** as a green-manure/shade crop; the shade
  regulates the thrips population.
- **Avoid growing chilli after sorghum** — the crop is more susceptible to thrips in that sequence.
- **Avoid a chilli + onion mixed crop**, because both crops host thrips and the mix concentrates
  the pest.
- **Sprinkling water over nursery seedlings checks thrips multiplication** — a free, low-input,
  physically-acting measure that exploits the pest's intolerance of moisture.
- Grow tolerant types; TNAU lists G5, K2 and X 235.
(eagri ENTO 331 Lecture 22 — http://eagri.org/eagri50/ENTO331/lecture22/chillies/001.html)

### Natural enemies in India
Predaceous thrips **_Scolothrips indicus_** and **_Franklinothrips megalops_** — TNAU explicitly
recommends encouraging their activity in the field. Also *Orius* spp. anthocorid bugs, predatory
mites *Amblyseius* spp., coccinellids, and the fungi *Beauveria bassiana* and *Lecanicillium
lecanii* under humid conditions.
(eagri ENTO 331 Lecture 22 — http://eagri.org/eagri50/ENTO331/lecture22/chillies/001.html)

### Virus transmission
*S. dorsalis* is reported as a vector of **tospoviruses**, including **Groundnut bud necrosis virus
(GBNV)** and **Capsicum chlorosis virus (CaCV)**, and it is associated with **Chilli leaf curl**
symptom complexes. ⚠ Its efficiency as a tospovirus vector is debated in the literature; the
dominant GBNV vector in India is *Thrips palmi* and the peanut/tomato tospovirus vector is
*Frankliniella schultzei*. Report *S. dorsalis* primarily as a direct-feeding-damage pest.

## 5. Yellow mite / broad mite / "muranai" mite — *Polyphagotarsonemus latus* (Acari: Tarsonemidae)

### What it is
A **mite, not an insect** — eight legs in the adult, four pairs, and no wings at any stage. This
matters because the biology (very short cycle, extreme sensitivity to humidity, dispersal by
phoresy on whiteflies) is different from an insect pest, and because it is invisible to the naked
eye — **diagnosis is made from the symptom, not from seeing the pest**.

### Life cycle and stage durations
- **Egg**: minute, oval, **laid on the ventral (lower) surface of young leaves or on leaf buds**;
  the egg surface carries characteristic white tubercles. **Egg period 1.5–2 days.**
- **Larva**: has **3 pairs of legs**, moves sluggishly. **Larval period 1.5 days.**
- **Nymph (quiescent pseudo-pupal stage)**: **1 day.**
- **Adult**: **0.1 mm long, with 4 pairs of legs, yellowish-green and translucent.**
  **Adult period 8–10 days.**
- **Total cycle roughly 4–5 days from egg to adult** — one of the fastest of any crop pest, which
  is why populations explode within a week and why scouting must be weekly or better.
(eagri ENTO 331 Lecture 22 — http://eagri.org/eagri50/ENTO331/lecture22/chillies/002.html)

### Where to look
**Only on the youngest growth** — the newest 2–3 unopened or just-opened leaves at the tip, the
underside, and the flower buds. Broad mite abandons tissue as it ages. If you look at old leaves
you will find nothing and wrongly conclude the mite is absent.

### Damage and diagnosis
Sudden **curling and crinkling of leaves, curling DOWNWARD**, followed by **blister patches** on
severely attacked plants. The **petiole becomes elongated — the "rat tail" symptom** — which is
diagnostic and unmistakable. Later growth stops and plants die. On chilli this is the "murda"
complex; the mite injects a growth-regulator-like toxin, so a **small number of mites causes
disproportionate distortion**.
(eagri ENTO 331 Lecture 22 — http://eagri.org/eagri50/ENTO331/lecture22/chillies/002.html)

### Seasonal peak and weather
Favoured by **warm, humid conditions** — roughly **25–30 °C with relative humidity above 70–80 %**
— and by shade and dense canopy. In India populations rise during and just after the monsoon and
in humid protected cultivation (polyhouse chilli, capsicum, gerbera). ⚠ Broad mite prefers humidity
whereas *Scirtothrips* prefers dry heat; the same field can flip from one to the other as weather
changes, so re-diagnosis at each visit is necessary.

### Off-season survival and host range
Extremely polyphagous (the name means "many-host tarsonemid"): chilli, capsicum, brinjal, tomato,
potato, okra, cotton, jute, mulberry, citrus, tea, coffee, rubber, castor, beans, papaya, mango,
and ornamentals including gerbera, chrysanthemum, jasmine and rose. It survives on these hosts and
on weeds continuously — there is no diapause.

### How it spreads
- Crawling between touching leaves and plants.
- **Phoresy**: broad mite females attach themselves to the legs of **whitefly adults (*Bemisia
  tabaci*)** and are flown to new plants. This is a well-documented dispersal route and it means a
  whitefly problem and a broad mite problem often arrive together.
- On **nursery seedlings and planting material** — the commonest way a new polyhouse or field is
  infested.
- On **workers' clothing, hands and tools** moving through an infested block.

### Natural enemies in India
Predatory mite **_Amblyseius ovalis_** — named explicitly by TNAU as the agent to encourage. Also
*Neoseiulus (Amblyseius) californicus*, *Amblyseius swirskii*, *Euseius* spp., predatory
staphylinid beetles and *Stethorus* spp. Conserve by avoiding broad-spectrum treatments that
remove phytoseiid mites; the removal of predatory mites is a classic cause of broad mite and spider
mite outbreaks.
(eagri ENTO 331 Lecture 22 — http://eagri.org/eagri50/ENTO331/lecture22/chillies/002.html)

### Virus transmission
No plant virus is known to be transmitted by *P. latus*. The damage is caused entirely by the
**phytotoxic saliva** injected during feeding, which is why symptoms persist and continue to
develop on new growth for days after the mite population has fallen.

## 6. Brinjal shoot and fruit borer — *Leucinodes orbonalis* (Lepidoptera: Crambidae/Pyraustidae)

### Status
India, Bangladesh, Malaysia, Thailand, Myanmar, Sri Lanka, Laos, South Africa, Congo. **A major and
regular pest of brinjal causing damage to 30–50 % of fruits or more.** It is the single most
important constraint in Indian brinjal.
(eagri ENTO 331 Lecture 21 — http://eagri.org/eagri50/ENTO331/lecture21/brinjal/001.html)

### Life cycle and stage durations
- **Egg**: **creamy white, laid singly** on **leaves, tender shoots, flowers and developing fruits**
  — a female lays **about 150–350 eggs**. **Egg period 3–4 days.**
- **Larva**: stout, **pink coloured**, with sparsely distributed hairs on warts and a brownish
  head. **Larval period 15 days, in 5 instars.** The larva **bores inside** the shoot or fruit
  within hours of hatching — this concealed habit is the central management problem, because once
  inside, the larva is protected from anything applied to the plant surface.
- **Pupa**: **6–8 days** in a **tough, greyish, boat-shaped cocoon on the plant itself** (also in
  fallen leaves and plant debris on the ground).
- **Adult**: medium-sized moth with **white wings flushed with triangular brown and red markings on
  the forewing**. Nocturnal.
- **Total life cycle: 17–50 days.**
(eagri ENTO 331 Lecture 21 — http://eagri.org/eagri50/ENTO331/lecture21/brinjal/001.html)

### Where each stage sits — what to look for and destroy
- Eggs: underside of young leaves, on flower buds and on the calyx of small fruits.
- Larvae early in the season: **inside the tender terminal shoot**, which then wilts and droops —
  the **"dead heart"**. Later in the season larvae move to fruits.
- Larvae in fruits: entry hole is **plugged with excreta (frass)**, so an infested fruit looks
  sound from a distance but shows a small dark plugged hole and internal tunnelling.
- Pupae: on the plant in a boat-shaped cocoon, and in fallen plant debris.
**Therefore the two mechanical actions with the highest return are: clip and destroy wilted shoots
weekly, and collect and destroy every bored and fallen fruit.** Removing the borer while it is
inside removes the next generation.

### Damage symptoms
Larva bores into tender shoots causing withering of terminal shoots and dead hearts; also bores the
petioles of leaves, flower buds and developing buds, causing withering of leaves and shedding of
buds; makes fruits unfit for consumption. Attacked fruits carry bore holes plugged with excreta and
become misshapen.
(eagri ENTO 331 Lecture 21)

### ETL
**1–5 % fruit damage.** (eagri ENTO 331 Lecture 21 —
http://eagri.org/eagri50/ENTO331/lecture21/brinjal/001.html)
This is a very low threshold because the crop is picked for fresh market and cosmetic damage is
total loss.

### Host range and alternate hosts
Brinjal (primary), potato, peas, and **other wild plants belonging to Solanaceae** — including
*Solanum melongena* wild relatives, *Solanum torvum*, *Solanum nigrum* and *Solanum indicum*.
Removing solanaceous weeds from bunds removes the off-season refuge.
(eagri ENTO 331 Lecture 21)

### Seasonal peak and weather
Active throughout the year wherever brinjal is grown; peaks in **warm, humid conditions**, broadly
**July to October** in most of India, with a second peak in **February to April**. Damage is
highest during the fruiting phase. ⚠

### Off-season survival
Survives as larvae and pupae in **crop residues, in leftover fruits, in ratooned brinjal plants**,
and on solanaceous wild hosts. **Avoiding continuous cropping of brinjal and avoiding ratooning**
is the specific TNAU recommendation and it works by removing this bridge.
(eagri ENTO 331 Lecture 21)

### Spread
Adult moth flight between neighbouring fields, generally short-range (tens to a few hundred
metres); transport of infested fruit moves larvae to new districts.

### Natural enemies in India
- **Larval parasitoid *Trathala flavoorbitalis*** (Ichneumonidae) — the most important, commonly
  giving substantial natural parasitism in unsprayed brinjal.
- *Pristomerus* spp., *Eriborus* spp., *Bracon* (= *Habrobracon*) *hebetor*, *Cremastus* spp.
- Egg parasitoid *Trichogramma chilonis* — relevant because eggs are laid exposed on the plant
  surface before the larva bores in; the **egg stage is the only window when the pest is exposed**.
- Predators: spiders, *Chrysoperla zastrowi sillemi*, reduviid bugs.
- Conserve by avoiding broad-spectrum treatment during the flowering and early fruiting window and
  by keeping flowering border plants for adult parasitoid nectar.

### Monitoring
**Pheromone traps** using the *L. orbonalis* sex pheromone at about **5 traps per hectare** for
monitoring (higher densities for mass trapping) give the moth-emergence signal; **light traps at
1 per hectare** attract and kill moths (eagri ENTO 331 Lecture 21). Grafting brinjal onto
*Solanum torvum* rootstock does not affect borer but is used against bacterial wilt — keep the two
problems separate in diagnosis.

### Cultural package (TNAU)
Avoid continuous cropping of brinjal and ratooning; grow tolerant varieties (Annamalai, Pusa Purple
Round, Pusa Purple Long, Arka Kusumakar, Doli-5, Chaklasi Doli, SM 67, SM 68, Pant Samrat); collect
and destroy damaged tender shoots, fallen fruits and fruits with bore holes to prevent population
build-up; use light traps at 1 per hectare.
(eagri ENTO 331 Lecture 21 — http://eagri.org/eagri50/ENTO331/lecture21/brinjal/001.html)

### Virus transmission
None. *L. orbonalis* is a chewing borer and transmits no plant virus.

## 7. Spotted bollworm — *Earias vittella* and *Earias insulana* (Lepidoptera: Nolidae; older texts Noctuidae)

### Status and hosts
Cosmopolitan; a major pest. Host range: **cotton, okra (bhendi), hollyhock, *Hibiscus cannabinus*
(mesta/kenaf), *Abutilon indicum***. All hosts are **Malvaceae** — this single fact drives the
whole cultural strategy: **okra is the bridge crop that carries *Earias* between cotton seasons**,
and *Abutilon indicum* is the wild reservoir.
(eagri ENTO 331 Lecture 11 — http://eagri.org/eagri50/ENTO331/lecture11/001.html)

### Life cycle and stage durations
- **Egg**: **sculptured, crown-shaped, deep sky-blue**, deposited **singly on the shoot tips, buds,
  flowers and fruits**. The blue crown-shaped egg is diagnostic and can be seen with a hand lens.
  **Egg period 3 days.**
- **Larva**: brown, with a **white median longitudinal streak** along the dorsum; the last two
  thoracic segments and all abdominal segments carry **two pairs of fleshy finger-shaped tubercles**
  (one dorsal, one lateral) in *E. insulana*; *E. vittella* is **without** these finger-shaped
  processes. **Larval period 10–12 days.**
- **Pupa**: **pupation is OUTSIDE the boll**, in a **tough, boat-shaped, dirty-white silken cocoon**
  — on the plant, in fallen debris and on the soil surface. **Pupal period 7–10 days.**
- **Adult**: *E. vittella* has **green forewings with a white streak** in each; *E. insulana* has
  **completely green forewings**.
- **Total cycle roughly 20–25 days**; multiple overlapping generations through the season.
(eagli ENTO 331 Lecture 11 — http://eagri.org/eagri50/ENTO331/lecture11/001.html and /002.html)

### Damage sequence — why timing of scouting changes with crop stage
Early in the season, when the crop is a few weeks old, the newly hatched caterpillar **leads a free
life for a few hours** — this is the only exposed window — then **bores into the top tender shoot**.
The shoot above the damage withers, droops and dries: **the terminal shoot "dead heart" of young
cotton and okra**. Depending on locality, up to **50 % of the crop may be damaged in this way**.
When squares and bolls begin to develop, the caterpillars **move from the shoots to the bolls**,
making **conspicuous holes**. Injured squares and small bolls **drop**; infested bolls that stay on
the plant are hollowed, filled with excrement, and **open prematurely and badly**.
(eagri ENTO 331 Lecture 11 — http://eagri.org/eagri50/ENTO331/lecture11/001.html)

**Field rule:** in the first 45 days look for **drooping shoot tips**; after square formation look
for **shed squares and holed bolls**. Because the entry hole is conspicuous and open (unlike pink
bollworm), *Earias* damage is visible from outside.

### Seasonal peak
Damage on okra runs through the whole okra season; on cotton the shoot-boring phase is early
(vegetative) and the boll phase from square formation onward. Peak in **July to October** in most
of India. ⚠

### Off-season survival
Survives on **okra, hollyhock, mesta and *Abutilon indicum*** and in cotton stalks and debris. It
has **no strong diapause** and depends on continuously available malvaceous hosts — hence the TNAU
instruction to **avoid other malvaceous crops in the vicinity of the cotton crop**, and to
**remove and destroy crop residues** to stop carry-over.
(eagri ENTO 331 Lecture 11 — http://eagri.org/eagri50/ENTO331/lecture11/003.html)

### Natural enemies in India
Egg parasitoid *Trichogramma chilonis*; egg-larval parasitoid **_Chelonus blackburnii_** — named
explicitly in the TNAU cotton package; larval parasitoids *Bracon (Habrobracon) brevicornis*,
*Bracon hebetor*, *Rogas aligarhensis*; predators *Chrysoperla zastrowi sillemi*, coccinellids,
spiders, reduviids and *Geocoris*. Conserve by intercropping with pulses (cowpea, greengram,
blackgram, soybean), which the TNAU package records as raising spider and coccinellid activity and
lowering bollworm incidence.
(eagri ENTO 331 Lecture 11 — http://eagri.org/eagri50/ENTO331/lecture11/002.html and /003.html)

### Bt cotton
The incidence of American bollworm, pink bollworm and spotted bollworm is low on Bt cotton. Bt
cotton protects against the bollworm group; the sucking pest complex — aphid, whitefly, mealybug —
and *Spodoptera litura* are managed by the cultural, biological and monitoring measures described
in their own sections.
(eagri ENTO 331 Lecture 10 — http://eagri.org/eagri50/ENTO331/lecture10/index.html)

### Virus transmission
None.

## 8. Pink bollworm — *Pectinophora gossypiella* (Lepidoptera: Gelechiidae)

### Why it is the hardest cotton pest to see
The larva bores into the boll and then **closes the aperture through which it entered**, so the
outside of an infested boll looks identical to a healthy one. Damage is discovered only when bolls
are cut open or at ginning. Advisory logic must therefore rely on **pheromone trap catches, rosette
flowers and destructive boll sampling**, not on visible external symptoms.
(eagri ENTO 331 Lecture 11 — http://eagri.org/eagri50/ENTO331/lecture11/003.html)

### Damage symptoms
Caterpillars feed on flower buds and flowers and bore into bolls. In flower buds they feed on the
developing anther and style and occasionally the ovary. **When larvae are inside a flower, the
flower fails to open and takes on a twisted "rosette" appearance** — the rosette flower is the
single best early field indicator. Young attacked bolls are shed after a few days; larger bolls
remain on the plant. **Locules are damaged, interlocular burrowing occurs, seeds are destroyed and
the lint is stained** — the loss is in both seed and fibre quality, not only in weight.
(eagri ENTO 331 Lecture 11 — http://eagri.org/eagri50/ENTO331/lecture11/003.html)

### Life cycle and stage durations
- **Egg**: laid singly or in small groups **under the calyx (bract) of green bolls, on flower buds
  and on the underside of young leaves**. **Egg period 3–5 days.** ⚠ (Stage durations are not given
  in the TNAU page; these are the standard published Indian figures — ICAR-CICR.)
- **Larva**: young larva white, late instars develop the characteristic **pink transverse bands**;
  colour varies from almost black, brown or green to pale or pink with alternating dark and light
  bands running the length of the body. **Larval period 8–21 days** in active development, in
  4 instars, entirely **inside the flower bud or boll**.
- **Pupa**: **7–10 days**, in the soil, in surface trash, in the boll or among seeds/lint.
- **Adult**: a small moth, brown or dull yellow or olive-grey with dark spots on the forewing,
  with fringed hind wings. Adult longevity about 1–2 weeks; a female lays roughly 100–200 eggs.
- **Total cycle about 25–35 days** in warm weather; **4–6 generations per cotton season**.
(Bionomics description: eagri ENTO 331 Lecture 11 —
http://eagri.org/eagri50/ENTO331/lecture11/003.html; stage durations from ICAR-CICR —
https://cicr.org.in/)

### Diapause — the defining feature of this pest
Pink bollworm has a **facultative larval diapause**. Late in the season, as day length shortens and
the crop matures, a large fraction of larvae enter a **resting (diapausing) state inside seeds, in
double seeds joined by silk, in unopened bolls, in gin trash, and in crop debris and soil**. Larvae
can remain in diapause for **6 months and up to 2 years** in stored seed, gin trash and field
debris. ⚠ Maximum diapause duration varies between reports; 6–12 months is the routinely cited
figure and up to about 2 years is reported.

**Everything about pink bollworm management follows from this one fact:**
- The pest is carried to the next season **inside seed and inside stored/unginned cotton**, not on
  the growing plant.
- **Destroying cotton stalks and disposing of crop residues as soon as harvest is over**, and
  **avoiding staking stalks in the field**, removes the diapausing population.
- **Avoiding ratoon and a double cotton crop** and **avoiding continuous cropping in winter and
  summer in the same area** denies the emerging moths a host.
- **Ginning mills and seed stores are epidemiologically part of the field** — carry-over from gin
  trash re-seeds a district.
(eagri ENTO 331 Lecture 11 — http://eagri.org/eagri50/ENTO331/lecture11/003.html)

### Where each stage sits — what to destroy
Eggs under the boll calyx; larvae inside squares, rosetted flowers and bolls; diapausing larvae
inside seed, double seeds, unopened bolls, gin trash and field debris; pupae in soil and trash.
**Crush pink bollworm larvae in the rosette flowers** and **collect and destroy shed fruiting parts
at weekly intervals** — both are explicit TNAU recommendations.
(eagri ENTO 331 Lecture 11)

### Monitoring and ETL
- **Pheromone traps (gossyplure) at 12 traps per hectare** to monitor adult moth activity — this is
  the TNAU figure, and the same density is used for *Spodoptera litura* and *Helicoverpa armigera*
  with their species-specific lures.
- **Action threshold: 8 moths per trap per night for 3 consecutive nights**, or **10 % rosette
  flowers**, or **10 % green boll damage** on destructive sampling of 20 bolls from 20 plants.
  ⚠ These are the ICAR-CICR/NCIPM operational thresholds; the TNAU page gives the trap density but
  not the numeric moth threshold.
- **Light traps (modified Robinson type with 125 W mercury lamps)** for prevalence and population
  fluctuation.
(eagri ENTO 331 Lecture 11 — http://eagri.org/eagri50/ENTO331/lecture11/003.html)

### Host range
Effectively **monophagous on Malvaceae**, principally cotton; also okra, hollyhock and *Hibiscus*
species. This narrow host range is what makes a **host-free period** so powerful against pink
bollworm — unlike whitefly, it cannot wait out the gap on unrelated crops.

### Seasonal peak
Damage rises steeply from **boll formation onward**, typically **October to January** in central and
south India and in the late season generally. Long-duration and late-sown crops carry the highest
damage, because the crop is still fruiting when the population has built up through several
generations.

### Spread
Adult moth flight over short to moderate distances; **long-distance spread is by human movement of
seed cotton, ginning trash and planting seed**. Using **acid-delinted seed** removes larvae that
travel with fuzz on seed.

### Resistance management (Bt cotton context)
Pink bollworm in India has developed **field resistance to Cry1Ac and to Cry2Ab** Bt proteins in
several regions. The biological reason is straightforward: with a narrow host range, a long
season, several generations per season, and no untreated refuge, **almost every individual in the
population is exposed to the same single selection pressure every generation, so resistant
individuals mate mainly with each other**. The counter-measures that follow from the biology are
**short-duration varieties and timely crop termination** (fewer generations exposed), **a strict
host-free period**, **synchronised area-wide sowing**, **refuge-in-bag compliance**, and **rotation
of intervention modes of action**. Growing one variety throughout a village and completing sowing
within 10–15 days are the TNAU expressions of the same principle.
(eagri ENTO 331 Lecture 11 — http://eagri.org/eagri50/ENTO331/lecture11/003.html; ICAR-CICR —
https://cicr.org.in/)

### Natural enemies in India
Because the larva is concealed inside the boll for almost its whole life, natural enemy impact is
lower than for exposed pests. Recorded agents: *Trichogramma* spp. on eggs, *Bracon
(Habrobracon) hebetor* and *Bracon brevicornis*, *Chelonus blackburnii*, *Apanteles* spp.,
*Elasmus johnstoni*; predators *Chrysoperla zastrowi sillemi*, ants, and spiders which take moths
and newly hatched larvae. Conserve by intercropping with pulses and by need-based intervention.

### Virus transmission
None.

## 9. American bollworm / gram pod borer / tomato fruit borer — *Helicoverpa armigera* (Lepidoptera: Noctuidae)

### Why it matters
One insect species attacking cotton, chickpea, pigeonpea, tomato, chilli, sorghum, maize, sunflower,
groundnut and many vegetables — so the same advice reaches farmers across almost every cropping
system. TNAU treats "chilli fruit borer" and "gram pod borer" as the same species, referring the
reader back to the cotton entry.
(eagri ENTO 331 Lecture 22 — http://eagri.org/eagri50/ENTO331/lecture22/chillies/004.html)

### Host range
Cotton, sorghum, lablab, pea, chillies, groundnut, tobacco, okra, maize, tomato, soybean,
safflower, chickpea (gram), pigeonpea (redgram), sunflower, and many more — over 180 plant species.
(eagri ENTO 331 Lecture 06 — http://eagri.org/eagri50/ENTO331/lecture06/001.html)

### Life cycle and stage durations
- **Egg**: laid **singly on the host plant**, on the youngest tissue — growing points, flower buds,
  tender leaves and the calyx of developing fruit. Spherical, ribbed, creamy white turning brown.
  A female lays roughly **500–1000 eggs** over her life. **Egg period 7 days.** ⚠ Under warm Indian
  field conditions the egg period is often reported as 2–4 days; the TNAU teaching figure of 7 days
  is a laboratory average.
- **Larva**: full-grown larva about 2 inches (**40–50 mm**) long, greenish with dark brown-grey
  lines and dark and pale longitudinal bands; **colour is highly variable from greenish to brown to
  almost black**, so colour is unreliable for identification. The reliable characters are the
  **light and dark longitudinal stripes** and the **rough skin with microspines** (visible as a
  gritty texture under a lens). **Larval duration 14 days**, in 5–6 instars.
- **Pupa**: **pupates in the SOIL**, in an earthen cell, at a depth of about **2.5–17 cm**.
  **Pupal period 10 days.**
- **Adult**: moth greenish to brown with a **'V'-shaped speck on the forewings** and a **dull black
  border on the hind wing**. Nocturnal, strong flier.
- **One generation is completed in 28 days under favourable conditions.**
  **About 8 generations per year** in India, overlapping. ⚠
(eagri ENTO 331 Lecture 06 — http://eagri.org/eagri50/ENTO331/lecture06/001.html)

### Where each stage sits — and what this means for action
- Eggs and small larvae: **on the surface of the youngest growth** — this is the exposed window and
  the only stage reachable by egg parasitoids and by anything applied to the plant.
- Larger larvae: **head thrust inside the pod/boll/fruit with the rest of the body hanging outside**
  — the classic diagnostic posture in pulses. Boreholes on pods, absent seeds inside pods, and
  early-stage defoliation are the symptoms.
- **Pupae in the soil** — this is the key. **Deep summer ploughing exposes pupae to the sun, to
  desiccation and to birds**, and is the single most effective off-season action against
  *Helicoverpa*. It works because the vulnerable stage is in the soil, not on the plant.
(eagri ENTO 331 Lecture 06 — http://eagri.org/eagri50/ENTO331/lecture06/001.html)

### Off-season survival
Pupae in the soil enter a **facultative diapause** in cool or dry conditions, allowing the
population to bridge the gap between crops. In warm parts of India generations are continuous on a
sequence of hosts: cotton → pigeonpea/chickpea → tomato → chilli → sunflower. The pest's ability to
move across this crop sequence is why **area-wide, cropping-system-level thinking beats field-level
thinking**.

### Seasonal peak and weather
- On **chickpea and pigeonpea**: peak damage **December to February**, at the pod initiation and
  pod filling stage.
- On **cotton**: **August to October**.
- On **tomato and chilli**: through the fruiting phase, **October to March**.
- **Warm, dry weather after a rain event favours build-up**; the moths are strong migrants and
  arrive on wind fronts. Cloudy, humid weather favours the natural NPV epizootic that often
  collapses populations.

### ETL
- **Pulses (redgram/chickpea): 1 larva per 5 plants at the pod initiation stage.**
  (eagri ENTO 331 Lecture 06 — http://eagri.org/eagri50/ENTO331/lecture06/001.html)
- **Cotton: an action threshold of 1 egg per plant or 1 larva per plant.**
  (eagri ENTO 331 Lecture 11 — http://eagri.org/eagri50/ENTO331/lecture11/002.html)
- **Tomato: 1 egg or 1 larva per plant**, or **5 % fruit damage**. ⚠
- **Pheromone traps at 12 per hectare** for monitoring adult moth activity; **8–10 moths per trap
  per night for 3 consecutive nights** signals the start of oviposition. ⚠
(eagri ENTO 331 Lecture 06 and Lecture 11)

### Spread
Adults are **strong, long-distance migrants**, flying tens to hundreds of kilometres on wind
systems, which is why an outbreak can appear in a region with no local source. Larvae move only
short distances between adjacent plants.

### Natural enemies in India — named
- **Egg parasitoids**: *Trichogramma chilonis*, *T. pretiosum*, *Trichogrammatoidea bactrae*.
  Inundative release of *Trichogramma* spp. is the standard recommendation, timed to egg-laying
  detected by pheromone traps.
- **Egg-larval parasitoid**: **_Chelonus blackburnii_** — named explicitly in the TNAU package.
- **Larval parasitoids**: *Campoletis chlorideae* (the most important in Indian pulses),
  *Carcelia illota*, *Goniophthalmus halli*, *Eucelatoria bryani*, *Bracon (Habrobracon) hebetor*.
- **Pupal parasitoid**: *Tetrastichus* spp.
- **Predators**: *Chrysoperla zastrowi sillemi* (green lacewing), coccinellids *Coccinella
  septempunctata* and *Cheilomenes sexmaculata*, reduviid bugs *Rhynocoris* spp., the pentatomid
  *Eocanthecona furcellata*, ants, and **spiders**. **Birds** are major predators of larvae.
- **Entomopathogens**: ***Helicoverpa armigera* nucleopolyhedrovirus (HaNPV)** — a naturally
  occurring virus specific to *Helicoverpa*, applied in the evening because it is inactivated by
  ultraviolet light; *Nomuraea rileyi*, *Beauveria bassiana*, *Metarhizium anisopliae*, and
  *Bacillus thuringiensis* var. *kurstaki*.
- **Conservation measures explicitly recommended**:
  - **Bird perches at 50 per hectare** so that insectivorous birds pick larvae off the crop — free,
    highly effective, and specific to this pest's exposed feeding habit.
  - **Intercropping and border cropping** — pulses (cowpea, greengram, blackgram, soybean) with
    cotton raise spider and coccinellid activity; **marigold as a trap crop** in tomato is
    standard, because *Helicoverpa* prefers marigold flowers for oviposition, concentrating eggs
    where they can be destroyed.
  - **Sorghum or maize border rows** provide a perch and shelter for predators.
  - Need-based, ETL-driven intervention and avoidance of broad-spectrum synthetic pyrethroid use,
    which the TNAU package identifies as a driver of resurgence.
(eagri ENTO 331 Lecture 06 — http://eagri.org/eagri50/ENTO331/lecture06/001.html; Lecture 11 —
http://eagri.org/eagri50/ENTO331/lecture11/002.html)

### Resistance
*H. armigera* is one of the world's most resistance-prone insects: high fecundity, many
generations per year, polyphagy across an entire cropping system, and long-distance migration that
mixes selected populations across regions. The TNAU package states the principle directly:
**alternate different groups of insecticides for each round of spray; avoid tank-mix combinations;
apply only when the population crosses ETL**. Rotating modes of action ensures survivors of one
selection pressure meet a different one in the next generation.
(eagri ENTO 331 Lecture 11 — http://eagri.org/eagri50/ENTO331/lecture11/002.html)

### Virus transmission
None. *Helicoverpa* is a chewing pest and transmits no plant virus.

## 10. Fall armyworm — *Spodoptera frugiperda* (Lepidoptera: Noctuidae)

### Status in India
An invasive species native to the Americas, **first recorded in India in May 2018 on maize in
Shivamogga, Karnataka**, and now established across the whole country. It attacks maize most
severely but has an enormous host range.

### Host range
**Over 353 plant species from 76 families**, dominated by **Poaceae (106 species), Asteraceae
(31) and Fabaceae (31)**. Confirmed Indian hosts beyond maize include **sugarcane, paddy, ginger,
bajra (pearl millet), sorghum, cotton, Johnson grass, sunflower, banana, fodder grasses, grain
amaranth and onion**.
(ICAR IPM module evaluation, *Scientific Reports* —
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10966016/; onion invasion report, *Frontiers in Ecology
and Evolution* — https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/ecology-and-evolution/articles/10.3389/fevo.2023.1279640/full)

Two strains exist: the **"corn strain" (C-strain)** favouring maize, cotton and sorghum, and the
**"rice strain" (R-strain)** favouring rice and pasture grasses. The Maharashtra onion population
was identified as the **R-strain**, demonstrating host adaptability across crops.
(Frontiers in Ecology and Evolution, 2023)

### Life cycle and stage durations — ICAR-IIMR observations, Hyderabad
- **Egg**: laid in **masses of 100–200 eggs, covered with buff/greyish scales from the female's
  abdomen**, giving a furry, mould-like appearance. Masses are placed on the **underside of leaves,
  near the base of the plant, and on the upper leaf surface in dense infestations**. **Incubation
  4 ± 0.45 days** (on onion); **2–3 days in hot weather**.
- **Larva — 6 instars**. ICAR-IIMR Winter Nursery Centre, Hyderabad, on maize:
  **total larval period 14.48 days** — I instar 2.55 d, II instar 2.12 d, III instar 2.08 d,
  IV instar 2.00 d, V instar 2.04 d, VI instar 3.69 d. On onion the total larval period was
  **22.2 ± 0.37 days**, showing that host quality strongly changes development speed.
- **Pupa**: **8.24 days** on maize; **8.0 ± 0.45 days** on onion. **Pupation is in the SOIL**, in a
  loose silk-and-soil cocoon at **2–8 cm depth**; in very hard or waterlogged soil the larva may
  pupate in the leaf whorl or in crop residue instead.
- **Adult**: longevity **12.6 days (female)** and **11.1 days (male)** on maize; **9.2 ± 0.37 d
  (female)** and **6.4 ± 0.40 d (male)** on onion.
- **Total life cycle 30.67–42.88 days**, observed from August to January under natural rearing at
  ICAR-IIMR, Hyderabad.
(Indian Journal of Entomology, biology of FAW on maize —
https://indianjournals.com/article/ije-80-3-003; ICAR-IIMR life-table study —
https://epubs.icar.org.in/index.php/IJAgS/article/view/101613)

### Identification — the four-marker rule
FAW larvae appear in shades of **green, olive, tan and grey**. Confirm with all four:
1. **Inverted white "Y" on the front of the dark head capsule.**
2. **Three creamy-yellow/white lines running down the back.**
3. **Four black spots (pinacula) on each abdominal segment.**
4. **On abdominal segment 8 the four black spots are larger and arranged in a SQUARE**; on segment
   9 they form a **trapezoid**. This tail-end square is the character that separates FAW from every
   other armyworm.
(Indian Journal of Entomology / ICAR-IIMR)

### Where to look — stage location and damage progression
- **Egg masses**: underside of leaves, low on the plant, furry-looking.
- **Neonates and early instars**: **scrape the leaf surface, leaving papery "windowpane" patches**
  that dry to elongated transparent windows. This is the earliest and most actionable symptom.
- **Mid and late instars**: move into the **whorl** and feed there, producing **ragged, elongated
  holes and copious moist sawdust-like frass in the whorl** — frass in the whorl is the single most
  reliable field sign.
- **Late instars**: bore into the **tassel and into developing cobs** through the husk or silk end.
- **Pupae in the soil at the base of the plant** — again making **deep ploughing after harvest** a
  high-value action.
- Cannibalism among larvae means usually **only 1–2 large larvae per whorl**, so a single larva per
  whorl is still a full infestation.

### Seasonal occurrence and weather
- Present year-round in southern and central India wherever maize is grown; **no diapause**, so
  survival depends on continuously available green hosts.
- **Highest incidence recorded in November** in the semi-arid Telangana demonstrations.
- **Warm temperatures (26–30 °C) with moderate humidity favour rapid development**; the pest
  completes generations fastest in hot weather.
- **Staggered and asynchronous sowing in a village sustains the population continuously**, which is
  why "avoid staggered sowing" is an explicit ICAR recommendation.
- Heavy rainfall drowns larvae in the whorl and reduces populations.
(https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10966016/)

### Off-season survival
No diapause. It bridges seasons on **volunteer maize, ratoon sorghum and sugarcane, fodder grasses,
Johnson grass, and irrigated rabi maize**. Long-distance migration re-invades regions each season.
Destroying volunteer maize and grass hosts and deep-ploughing after harvest are the practical
levers.

### Spread
- **Adult moths are exceptional long-distance migrants**, capable of **100 km or more in a single
  night** on favourable winds, and several hundred kilometres over a generation. This is how the
  species crossed India within two years of arrival.
- **Larvae travel on green fodder, on maize cobs and on planting material.**
- **"Armyworm" marching**: when a field is stripped, larvae move en masse on the ground into the
  adjacent field. A **deep trench with steep sides dug between fields traps marching larvae** — a
  physical control that follows directly from this behaviour.

### Economic threshold / action threshold (stage-specific)
- **1–2 larvae per whorl**
- **5 % of seedlings cut**
- **15 % of whorls infested in young plants within the first 30 days**
- **Pheromone traps capturing more than 2–3 adult moths per trap for three consecutive days**
  triggers management initiation.
(https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10966016/)

Additional widely used Indian thresholds: **5 % foliar damage in the early whorl stage (up to 30
days)**, **10 % in the mid-whorl (30–60 days)**, and **10 % ear damage** at the tasselling and
cob stage. ⚠ Threshold reporting for FAW varies significantly between and within crops; a global
review notes that standardised economic thresholds are still lacking
(https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0261219421001113).

### Monitoring
**Pheromone traps at 8 traps with 40 lures per acre (approximately 20 traps per hectare)**,
deployed from the **7-day-old crop until harvest**, with **lures replaced every 21 days**. Lower
densities (about 5 traps/ha) serve for monitoring only; higher densities for mass trapping.
(https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10966016/)

### Natural enemies in India — named
- **Predators**: **_Eocanthecona furcellata_** (native predatory stink bug, Pentatomidae) —
  recorded attacking FAW larvae in India; also *Chrysoperla zastrowi sillemi*, *Coccinella
  septempunctata*, earwigs (*Forficula* spp.), ants (*Camponotus*, *Solenopsis*), ground beetles,
  reduviid bugs *Rhynocoris* spp., and spiders. **Birds** take larvae from whorls — hence **bird
  perches at 10 per acre (about 25 per hectare)** in the ICAR module.
- **Egg parasitoids**: *Telenomus remus* (the most important, recovered in India), *Trichogramma
  chilonis*, *Trichogramma pretiosum*.
- **Larval parasitoids recovered in India**: *Campoletis chlorideae*, *Coccygidium* (=*Therophilus*)
  *melleum*, *Glyptapanteles creatonoti*, *Chelonus formosanus*, *Odontepyris* sp., *Cotesia*
  spp., and the tachinids *Megaselia* sp. and *Peribaea* sp. ⚠ The Indian parasitoid complex
  assembled rapidly from native species after 2018 and continues to be described; state-level
  records vary.
- **Entomopathogens**: *Metarhizium anisopliae*, *Metarhizium rileyi* (= *Nomuraea rileyi*) — which
  produces spectacular natural epizootics on FAW in humid Indian conditions, turning larvae white
  or green and mummified — *Beauveria bassiana*, *Bacillus thuringiensis* var. *kurstaki*, and
  *S. frugiperda* NPV.
- **Conservation**: **clean cultivation with balanced fertiliser application**, **avoiding
  staggered sowing**, **manual egg mass collection and destruction**, **bird perches**, and
  intercropping maize with **pulses (cowpea, blackgram, greengram) or with pigeonpea**, which
  supports parasitoids and predators. Intercropping maize with legumes also lowers oviposition.
(https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10966016/; ICAR-NBAIR — https://www.nbair.res.in/)

### Yield impact
Maize yield reductions of **15–73 %** are recorded in Indian conditions.
(https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10966016/)

### Low-cost physical measures that follow from the biology
- **Placing dry sand, or sand mixed with lime, in the whorl** — the abrasive material kills larvae
  concealed in the whorl, exploiting the fact that the larva sits in a funnel that collects
  anything poured onto the plant.
- **Deep summer ploughing** to expose soil pupae to sun and birds.
- **Trenches between fields** to trap marching larvae.
- **Hand-picking and crushing egg masses** — they are large, conspicuous and furry.

### Virus transmission
None. FAW is a chewing pest and transmits no plant virus. Its wounds can, however, provide entry
points for **_Fusarium_ ear and stalk rots** in maize, which is a secondary disease consequence
worth flagging to farmers.

## 11. Black thrips — *Thrips parvispinus* (Thysanoptera: Thripidae)

### Status in India — a recent invasion
An invasive thrips of southeast Asian origin. It was **first recorded in India on papaya around
2015 in Bengaluru**, and then caused a **severe, widely reported outbreak on chilli in Andhra
Pradesh, Telangana and Karnataka from late 2021 into 2022**, spreading subsequently to Maharashtra,
Madhya Pradesh, Tamil Nadu and beyond. Because it arrived after the standard TNAU/eagri lecture
notes were written, it is absent from those and must be diagnosed on its own characters.
⚠ The Indian literature on this species is still developing; stage durations below are from
southeast Asian and early Indian studies and should be treated as approximate.
(ICAR-NBAIR pest alerts — https://www.nbair.res.in/; ICAR-IIHR — https://iihr.res.in/)

### Identification — separating it from *Scirtothrips dorsalis*
- ***Thrips parvispinus* adults are distinctly BICOLOURED — dark brown to blackish head and thorax
  with a paler yellowish abdomen in the female**, giving the field name **"black thrips"**.
- ***Scirtothrips dorsalis* adults are uniformly pale yellow to straw coloured.**
- The colour difference is visible with a hand lens and is the single practical field character.
- **Where they sit differs too**: *T. parvispinus* concentrates **inside flowers and on flower buds
  and terminal shoots**; *S. dorsalis* concentrates on **young leaves**.

### Life cycle and stage durations
- **Egg**: **inserted into plant tissue** — into flower parts, buds and tender leaves — so eggs are
  concealed. **Egg period about 4–5 days.** ⚠
- **Larva (2 instars)**: feeds on flowers and tender tissue. **Larval period about 5–7 days.** ⚠
- **Prepupa and pupa**: **passed in the soil, in leaf litter and in fallen flowers at the base of
  the plant**. **About 3–5 days combined.** ⚠
- **Adult**: longevity about **10–20 days**; fecundity roughly 40–60 eggs per female. ⚠
- **Total cycle approximately 14–20 days at warm temperature**, giving many overlapping
  generations. Reproduction includes **arrhenotokous parthenogenesis** — unmated females produce
  male offspring — so a single female can found a population.

### Damage and diagnosis — a flower pest, not a leaf pest
This is the crucial difference for advice. *T. parvispinus* feeds principally on **flowers, flower
buds and terminal growing points**. The result is:
- **Heavy flower drop and bud drop** — the dominant symptom, and the direct cause of yield loss.
- **Blackening and scarring of buds and petals.**
- **Downward curling and crinkling of terminal leaves**, with a brown/bronze scorched appearance,
  and stunted, bushy terminal growth.
- **Fruit set fails**, and set fruits are scarred and deformed.
Because damage centres on flowers, **the yield loss appears as "no fruit set" rather than as leaf
symptoms**, and farmers often misattribute it to nutrient deficiency, poor pollination or virus.

**Diagnostic triage for a chilli crop showing distortion:**
| Symptom pattern | Likely cause |
|---|---|
| Leaves curl **upward**, bronzed, plants stunted | *Scirtothrips dorsalis* (chilli thrips) |
| Leaves curl **downward**, crinkled, blistered, **elongated "rat-tail" petioles** | *Polyphagotarsonemus latus* (yellow/broad mite) |
| **Flower and bud drop**, blackened buds, dark bicoloured thrips in flowers | *Thrips parvispinus* (black thrips) |
| Vein clearing, mosaic, leaf curl, stunting, whiteflies present | Chilli leaf curl **begomovirus** via *Bemisia tabaci* |
| Necrotic rings/spots, bud necrosis, thrips present | **Tospovirus** (GBNV/CaCV) via thrips |

### Where to look
**Open a flower over a white sheet or a dark tray and tap it.** The adults fall out and are visible
as dark, fast-moving specks. Counting is done **per flower**, not per leaf — a departure from
standard thrips scouting that matters when giving instructions.

### Host range
Chilli and capsicum (primary in India), **papaya**, potato, tomato, brinjal, French bean, cotton,
watermelon and other cucurbits, mango, and a wide range of ornamentals including **_Gardenia_,
_Dahlia_, _Hoya_, _Schefflera_, _Ixora_, chrysanthemum, jasmine and gerbera**. Ornamental nurseries
are an important reservoir and a known route of international movement. ⚠

### Seasonal peak and weather
Populations rise strongly in **warm, dry weather** and during the **flowering phase**. In Indian
chilli the reported peaks are **October to February** in the rabi crop, with a build-up as the crop
enters flowering. Prolonged heavy rain and high humidity suppress numbers. ⚠

### Off-season survival
No diapause. Survives on the wide host range including papaya, ornamentals and weeds, and in
protected cultivation. Ornamental and nursery plants held under polyhouse conditions are the
year-round reservoir.

### Spread
Weak flight but strong wind-assisted dispersal; **long-distance spread is dominated by trade in
nursery seedlings, ornamental planting material and produce**. Farm workers moving between blocks
carry adults on clothing.

### Natural enemies in India
Predatory anthocorid bugs ***Orius* spp.** (notably *Orius maxidentex*), predatory mites
*Amblyseius swirskii* and *Neoseiulus* spp., predatory thrips *Franklinothrips* spp. and
*Scolothrips indicus*, and the entomopathogenic fungi *Beauveria bassiana*, *Metarhizium
anisopliae* and *Lecanicillium lecanii*. **Conserving *Orius* is especially valuable because it
hunts inside flowers, which is exactly where this thrips lives.** ⚠ Indian natural enemy records
for this species are still being compiled.

### Cultural principles that follow from the biology
- **Blue sticky traps** are more attractive to thrips than yellow; set at canopy height for early
  detection of flower-dwelling thrips.
- **Raise nursery under 40–50 mesh insect-proof net** and inspect purchased seedlings and
  ornamental planting material before bringing them onto the farm — the main invasion route.
- **Remove and destroy fallen flowers and crop debris**, because the pupal stage sits in litter and
  surface soil.
- **Deep ploughing between crops** reaches soil-dwelling pupae.
- **Maintain a host-free period** and avoid overlapping chilli plantings in a locality.
- **Border rows of maize or sorghum** act as a physical windbreak reducing thrips immigration.

### Virus transmission
⚠ *T. parvispinus* is **not confirmed** as an efficient vector of tospoviruses in India; the
principal Indian tospovirus vectors are *Thrips palmi* (Groundnut bud necrosis virus) and
*Frankliniella schultzei*. Treat *T. parvispinus* as a direct-damage pest whose loss is expressed
through flower and bud abscission. Its presence in a crop, however, frequently coincides with
*S. dorsalis* and *T. palmi*, so tospovirus risk should be assessed on symptoms independently.
