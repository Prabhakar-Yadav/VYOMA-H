## 1. PM-KISAN — Pradhan Mantri Kisan Samman Nidhi

**What the farmer gets**
Rupees 6,000 every year, paid straight into the bank account.
It comes in three instalments of Rupees 2,000 each.
The three instalment windows are April to July, August to November, and December to March.
The money is a direct benefit transfer. No repayment. No interest.

**Who qualifies**
A landholding farmer family — husband, wife and minor children — qualifies when farmland is recorded in their name in the village land records.
Both small and large landholding farmers qualify.
The land record must show the family as the owner.

Certain groups stay outside the scheme: institutional landholders, serving and retired government employees above Group D or Multi Tasking Staff level, income tax payers of the last assessment year, and practising doctors, engineers, lawyers, chartered accountants and architects.
Pensioners drawing a monthly pension of Rupees 10,000 or more also stay outside.

**How to apply**
1. Go to pmkisan.gov.in and open "New Farmer Registration". Or visit the nearest Common Service Centre. Or meet the village patwari, revenue officer or the State PM-KISAN Nodal Officer.
2. Enter the Aadhaar number and choose rural or urban farmer registration.
3. Fill land details exactly as they appear in the land record — survey number, khasra number, area.
4. Enter bank account and IFSC. The bank account must be linked to Aadhaar.
5. Complete e-KYC. Do this on the portal with an Aadhaar OTP, or at a CSC with a fingerprint, or through Face Authentication on the PM-KISAN Mobile App.
6. Track the application under "Know Your Status" using the registration number or mobile number.

**Documents needed**
- Aadhaar card
- Land record document — khatauni, khasra, RoR or 7/12 extract
- Bank passbook or account details with IFSC, Aadhaar-linked
- Mobile number
- Citizenship proof

**Deadline pattern**
Registration stays open all year.
The instalment a farmer receives depends on when verification finishes. A farmer verified inside a window receives that window's payment.
Completing e-KYC and Aadhaar-bank seeding keeps the payment flowing. Land seeding must also be marked "Yes" in the portal.

⚠ Instalment release dates shift. The exclusion list and the pension threshold are revised from time to time.

## 2. PMFBY — Pradhan Mantri Fasal Bima Yojana (Crop Insurance)

**What the farmer gets**
Insurance cover on the notified crop for the full sum insured.
Cover runs from sowing right through to post-harvest.
The farmer pays a small share of the premium. The Central and State Governments pay the rest.

Farmer premium share:
- Kharif food crops and oilseeds — 2 percent of sum insured
- Rabi food crops and oilseeds — 1.5 percent of sum insured
- Annual commercial and annual horticultural crops — 5 percent of sum insured

**What is covered**
- Prevented sowing when rainfall fails or conditions turn adverse
- Standing crop loss from drought, dry spell, flood, inundation, landslide, pest attack, disease, natural fire, lightning, storm, hailstorm, cyclone and tornado
- Post-harvest loss for up to 14 days for a crop cut and left to dry in the field
- Localised calamity — hailstorm, landslide, inundation, cloudburst and natural fire on an individual field

**Who qualifies**
Every farmer growing a notified crop in a notified area qualifies.
Sharecroppers and tenant farmers qualify when they can show insurable interest in the crop.
The scheme is voluntary for all farmers, including farmers with a crop loan.

**How to apply**
1. Choose the enrolment route — the pmfby.gov.in Farmer Corner, the Crop Insurance mobile app, a Common Service Centre, the bank branch that holds the crop loan account, or the empanelled insurance company agent.
2. Register with Aadhaar and mobile number.
3. Enter the season, the crop and the land details.
4. Pay the farmer premium share.
5. Collect the policy receipt. Under "Meri Policy Mere Haath", the policy document is delivered to the farmer.

Enrolment through a Common Service Centre carries no service charge for the farmer.

**Documents needed**
- Aadhaar card
- Bank passbook, Aadhaar-linked
- Land record — RoR, khasra or 7/12 extract
- Sowing certificate or self-declaration of sowing
- Tenancy agreement or a sharecropper declaration for tenant farmers

**Reporting a loss — the 72-hour rule**
When a localised or post-harvest loss happens, report it inside 72 hours.
Report through the Crop Insurance App, the pmfby.gov.in portal, the insurance company toll-free number, the bank, or the local agriculture officer.
Give the survey number and the affected area.
Helpline: **14447**.

**Deadline pattern**
⚠ Cut-off dates are notified season by season and differ by state and crop.
The common pattern is 31 July for Kharif and 31 December for Rabi.
Check the state notification each season. The notification also lists which crops are covered in which block.

## 3. Kisan Credit Card (KCC)

**What the farmer gets**
A running credit limit for crop production, farm maintenance and household needs.
The card also covers allied activity — dairy, poultry, goatery, piggery, beekeeping and fisheries.

Interest terms under the Modified Interest Subvention Scheme:
- Short-term farm credit up to Rupees 3 lakh carries a concessional rate of 7 percent per year.
- Repaying on time earns a further 3 percent Prompt Repayment Incentive.
- On-time repayment brings the effective rate down to 4 percent per year.

Loans up to Rupees 2 lakh are given without collateral. This limit rose from Rupees 1.60 lakh to Rupees 2 lakh with effect from 1 January 2025.

⚠ The Union Budget 2025-26 announced raising the KCC loan limit under the interest subvention scheme from Rupees 3 lakh to Rupees 5 lakh. Confirm the current ceiling with the bank branch.

The card is valid for 5 years, with the limit reviewed each year.
A KCC holder is covered under the Personal Accident Insurance Scheme where the bank offers it.

**Who qualifies**
- Farmers who own and cultivate land, on their own or jointly
- Tenant farmers, oral lessees and sharecroppers
- Members of Joint Liability Groups and Self Help Groups, including sharecropper groups
- Farmers in animal husbandry and fisheries, including fish farmers, fishers who own a boat, and people rearing dairy animals, poultry, sheep, goats and pigs

**How to apply**
1. Visit the bank branch that holds the account — commercial bank, regional rural bank, small finance bank or cooperative bank. Or apply online on the bank's website or through the PM-KISAN portal KCC link.
2. Fill the common one-page KCC application form.
3. Attach the documents.
4. The bank works out the limit from the scale of finance for the crop and the area cultivated.
5. The bank issues the card within the timeline set by the Reserve Bank of India.

The Kisan Rin Portal (KRP) and Ghar Ghar KCC campaigns run enrolment drives at the village level. Bank Mitras and CSCs help fill forms.

**Documents needed**
- Filled application form
- Identity proof — Aadhaar, voter ID, driving licence or passport
- Address proof
- Land record or a certificate of cultivation for tenant farmers
- Passport size photographs
- Bank account details

**Deadline pattern**
Applications are accepted all year.
Applying before the sowing season leaves time for the money to arrive when inputs are bought.
Repaying inside 12 months of drawal, or by the due date the bank sets, protects the 3 percent Prompt Repayment Incentive.

⚠ Interest rates, subvention ceilings and collateral-free limits are revised by RBI and the Government from time to time.

## 4. Soil Health Card Scheme

**What the farmer gets**
A printed card showing the nutrient status of the farmer's own soil.
The card carries crop-wise fertiliser recommendations and soil amendment advice.
Testing and the card are provided free to the farmer.

The card reports 12 parameters:
- Main nutrients — Nitrogen (N), Phosphorus (P), Potassium (K)
- Secondary nutrient — Sulphur (S)
- Micronutrients — Zinc (Zn), Iron (Fe), Copper (Cu), Manganese (Mn), Boron (B)
- Physical parameters — pH, Electrical Conductivity (EC), Organic Carbon (OC)

**Who qualifies**
Every farmer holding cultivable land qualifies. There is no landholding limit and no income limit.

**How to get soil tested**
1. Approach the village level soil testing lab, the Krishi Vigyan Kendra, the block agriculture office or the district soil testing laboratory.
2. Agriculture department staff or a trained village youth collect the sample with a GPS-tagged location.
3. Samples are collected on a grid — one sample for every 2.5 hectares in irrigated land, and one for every 10 hectares in rainfed land.
4. Sampling is done after harvest, when the field carries no standing crop. This usually happens twice a year, after Rabi harvest and after Kharif harvest.
5. The lab tests the sample and uploads results to the national Soil Health Card portal.
6. The card is handed to the farmer and is also available to download from soilhealth.dac.gov.in using the mobile number or the card number.

A farmer may also carry a properly drawn sample directly to the nearest soil testing lab or KVK.

**Documents needed**
- Aadhaar or another identity proof
- Land record or survey number
- Mobile number for the card to be linked and traced

**Deadline pattern**
Sampling runs in campaign mode after each harvest.
⚠ The scheme now runs as the Soil Health and Fertility component under the umbrella Rashtriya Krishi Vikas Yojana. Village level soil testing labs and mobile testing vans are being expanded, so the way a farmer books a test differs from state to state.

## 5. Pradhan Mantri Krishi Sinchayee Yojana (PMKSY)

PMKSY works through several components. A farmer usually meets it through micro-irrigation and watershed work.

### 5a. Per Drop More Crop (PDMC) — drip and sprinkler

**What the farmer gets**
Financial assistance to install a drip or sprinkler irrigation system.
- 55 percent of the cost for small and marginal farmers
- 45 percent of the cost for other farmers

Many states add a top-up on this. ⚠ The state top-up varies widely — some states take the farmer's share down sharply.

**Who qualifies**
Farmers who own or hold cultivable land with an assured water source qualify.
Assistance is normally allowed once for a given area, with a gap before the same area is covered again.

**How to apply**
1. Register on the state horticulture or agriculture department's micro-irrigation portal, or apply through the district horticulture or agriculture office.
2. Choose an empanelled micro-irrigation system supplier.
3. The department approves the work order after checking land and water source.
4. The supplier installs the system.
5. A department team inspects and verifies the installation.
6. The subsidy is paid into the farmer's bank account by direct benefit transfer.

**Documents needed**
- Aadhaar
- Land record
- Bank passbook
- Proof of water source, such as a bore well or pump certificate
- Caste certificate where a higher rate applies
- Supplier quotation and, later, the bill

**Deadline pattern**
⚠ States open application windows by season and by target. Apply early in the financial year, since targets fill up.

### 5b. Har Khet Ko Pani and Watershed Development
Support for canal command development, farm ponds, check dams, water harvesting structures and repair of village tanks.
These come as village or watershed level works. Approach the block office or the watershed committee.

### 5c. Micro Irrigation Fund with NABARD
A Rupees 5,000 crore fund with NABARD lends to state governments at a concessional rate so states can extend extra incentives for micro-irrigation.
This one reaches the farmer through the state top-up, rather than by direct application.

⚠ From 2022-23 the Per Drop More Crop component is implemented under the Rashtriya Krishi Vikas Yojana umbrella. The name on the state application form may differ.

## 6. Paramparagat Krishi Vikas Yojana (PKVY) — Organic Farming

**What the farmer gets**
Support of Rupees 31,500 per hectare over 3 years for farming organically in a cluster.
Of this:
- Rupees 15,000 per hectare goes straight to the farmer by direct benefit transfer for on-farm and off-farm organic inputs
- Rupees 4,500 per hectare covers marketing, packaging, branding and value addition
- Rupees 3,000 per hectare covers certification and residue analysis

Farmers also get training, exposure visits, and Participatory Guarantee System (PGS-India) organic certification.

**Who qualifies**
Farmers who join a cluster qualify. A cluster is formed of about 20 hectares, with roughly 50 farmers in the group.
Both small and large farmers may join.
The cluster commits to organic practice for the full 3-year conversion period.

**How to apply**
1. Talk to the block agriculture officer, the ATMA office or the Krishi Vigyan Kendra about the nearest PKVY cluster.
2. Join or help form a 20-hectare cluster with neighbouring farmers.
3. The cluster registers on the PGS-India portal (pgsindia-ncof.gov.in) through a Regional Council or the implementing agency.
4. Each farmer registers land parcels and keeps a field diary of inputs used.
5. Peer inspection happens within the group. The Regional Council issues the PGS green or organic certificate.
6. DBT assistance is released to the farmer's Aadhaar-linked account.

**Documents needed**
- Aadhaar
- Land record for every parcel entered into the cluster
- Bank passbook, Aadhaar-linked
- Group or cluster membership record

**Deadline pattern**
Clusters are sanctioned at the start of the financial year.
⚠ PKVY now runs under the Rashtriya Krishi Vikas Yojana umbrella, alongside the National Mission on Natural Farming. Assistance amounts and cluster size are revised from time to time, and the North Eastern states run a separate Mission Organic Value Chain Development scheme with different rates.

## 7. Agriculture Infrastructure Fund (AIF)

**What the farmer gets**
A medium and long term loan to build post-harvest and community farming infrastructure — warehouses, cold stores, grading and sorting units, pack houses, primary processing units, ripening chambers, assaying units, custom hiring centres and farm machinery banks.

On loans up to Rupees 2 crore:
- 3 percent interest subvention for up to 7 years
- Interest is capped at 9 percent per year
- Credit guarantee under CGTMSE, with the guarantee fee paid by the Government

**Who qualifies**
- Individual farmers
- Farmer Producer Organisations (FPOs)
- Primary Agricultural Credit Societies (PACS)
- Self Help Groups and Joint Liability Groups
- Marketing cooperative societies, multipurpose cooperative societies
- Agri-entrepreneurs and start-ups
- Central and State agencies, and Public Private Partnership projects

**How to apply**
1. Go to agriinfra.dac.gov.in.
2. Register with mobile number and Aadhaar number. An OTP creates the login.
3. Prepare a Detailed Project Report (DPR) for the proposed asset.
4. Fill the application form on the portal and upload the DPR.
5. Choose the lending bank from the list on the portal.
6. The Department verifies the project. The bank appraises and sanctions the loan.
7. The interest subvention is credited against the loan account each year.

**Documents needed**
- Aadhaar and PAN
- Detailed Project Report with cost estimate
- Land ownership or lease document for the project site
- Bank account details
- Registration certificate for an FPO, SHG, cooperative or company
- Quotations for machinery and civil work

**Deadline pattern**
Applications are open on the portal.
⚠ The scheme's sanction window and eligible activity list have been widened over time. Confirm the current eligible activity list on the portal before preparing a DPR.

## 8. e-NAM — National Agriculture Market

**What the farmer gets**
An online trading platform that links APMC mandis across the country into one market.
Buyers from many mandis and many states can bid for the same lot.
The lot is quality assayed before bidding, so the buyer bids on a known quality.
Payment comes directly into the farmer's bank account.
Registration is free.

The platform covers gate entry, lot creation with a unique lot ID, quality assaying, open online bidding, live bid tracking, price discovery and electronic payment.
Quality assaying is available for a large list of commodities using Directorate of Marketing and Inspection specifications.

**Who qualifies**
Any farmer bringing produce to an e-NAM linked mandi qualifies.
FPOs may trade from the collection centre without physically moving the lot to the mandi.

**How to apply**
1. Visit the nearest e-NAM linked mandi with documents. Or self-register at enam.gov.in and complete verification at the mandi.
2. The mandi issues a farmer registration ID.
3. Bring the produce to the mandi. Gate entry is recorded and a lot ID is created.
4. A sample is drawn and assayed. The assay report is displayed with the lot.
5. Buyers bid online. The farmer sees the bids and accepts or declines the highest bid.
6. Payment is released to the bank account. Some states allow a part of the payment in cash at the mandi.

**Documents needed**
- Aadhaar
- Bank passbook or a cancelled cheque
- Mobile number
- Land record or a produce ownership proof where the state asks for it

**Deadline pattern**
Trading runs on mandi working days.
Arriving early in the day gives the lot time for assaying before the bidding session.

⚠ The list of linked mandis, assayed commodities and the cash payment limit differ by state and keep growing.

## 9. Sub-Mission on Agricultural Mechanization (SMAM)

**What the farmer gets**
Subsidy to buy farm machinery and implements — tractors, power tillers, rotavators, seed drills, planters, threshers, harvesters, plant protection equipment and self-propelled machines.
Assistance is 40 to 50 percent of the machine cost, depending on the category of farmer.
Small and marginal farmers, women farmers, and farmers of Scheduled Caste and Scheduled Tribe communities get the higher rate.

For groups:
- Custom Hiring Centres and hi-tech hubs — 40 percent of project cost for rural entrepreneurs, cooperative societies, registered farmer societies, FPOs and panchayats
- Village level Farm Machinery Banks — 80 percent of project cost for projects up to Rupees 30 lakh, for cooperative societies, registered farmer societies, FPOs, Self Help Groups and panchayats

Machinery can also simply be hired through a Custom Hiring Centre, without buying anything. The FARMS app lists nearby centres and available machines.

**Who qualifies**
Individual farmers who own or cultivate land qualify for machinery subsidy.
Registered groups qualify for Custom Hiring Centres and Farm Machinery Banks.

**How to apply**
1. Register on agrimachinery.nic.in, or on the state's own farm mechanisation portal, or through the district agriculture office or the Common Service Centre.
2. Select the machine or implement from the approved list.
3. Submit the application within the state's application window. Many states allot by lottery when applications exceed the target.
4. Wait for the permit or work order.
5. Buy the machine from an empanelled dealer and keep the invoice.
6. A department officer physically verifies the machine and records the engine and chassis number.
7. The subsidy is credited to the bank account by direct benefit transfer.

**Documents needed**
- Aadhaar
- Land record
- Bank passbook, Aadhaar-linked
- Caste certificate where the higher subsidy rate applies
- Passport photograph
- Dealer quotation, and the invoice after purchase

**Deadline pattern**
⚠ States open and close application windows on their own calendar, often at the start of the financial year and again before the main sowing season. Targets are limited and fill quickly.

## 10. PM Kisan Maandhan Yojana (PM-KMY) — Farmer Pension

**What the farmer gets**
An assured pension of Rupees 3,000 per month from the age of 60.
The Central Government contributes the same amount into the pension fund as the farmer pays in.
If the pensioner dies, the spouse receives 50 percent of the pension as family pension.
If the subscriber dies before 60, the spouse may continue the scheme or take the accumulated amount with interest.

**What the farmer pays**
A monthly contribution between Rupees 55 and Rupees 200, fixed by the age at entry.
The younger the farmer at entry, the smaller the monthly contribution.
Contributions continue until the age of 60.

A PM-KISAN beneficiary may allow the contribution to be deducted from the PM-KISAN benefit itself, so nothing has to be paid out of pocket.

**Who qualifies**
Small and marginal farmers aged 18 to 40 years, with cultivable landholding up to 2 hectares recorded in the state land records, qualify.
Enrolment is voluntary.

Farmers already covered by other statutory social security schemes such as EPFO, ESIC or NPS, and income tax payers, are served by those schemes instead.

**How to apply**
1. Visit the nearest Common Service Centre with documents. Or register at maandhan.in. Or contact the State PM-KISAN Nodal Officer.
2. The Village Level Entrepreneur enters Aadhaar, name, date of birth, address, mobile number, bank account and nominee details.
3. The system calculates the monthly contribution from the age.
4. Pay the first contribution in cash at the CSC.
5. Sign the enrolment-cum-auto-debit mandate form.
6. Receive the Kisan Pension Account Number and the pension card.

**Documents needed**
- Aadhaar
- Land record showing landholding up to 2 hectares
- Savings bank account or Jan Dhan account passbook with IFSC
- Mobile number
- Nominee details

**Deadline pattern**
Enrolment is open all year, for anyone aged 18 to 40.
Enrolling early keeps the monthly contribution low, since the amount is fixed by entry age.

⚠ The contribution table and the eligibility land ceiling are set in the operational guidelines and may be revised.

## 11. Rashtriya Krishi Vikas Yojana (PM-RKVY)

**What it is**
An umbrella scheme through which the Centre gives states money for agriculture, and states design the projects.
From 2024-25 the Ministry's centrally sponsored schemes were reorganised into two umbrellas — Pradhan Mantri Rashtriya Krishi Vikas Yojana (PM-RKVY) for sustainable agriculture, and Krishonnati Yojana (KY) for food security and self-sufficiency.
PM-RKVY works as a cafeteria scheme. Each state chooses the components it needs and may move funds between components.

**What reaches the farmer**
Many familiar schemes now sit inside this umbrella, including:
- Soil Health and Fertility
- Per Drop More Crop micro-irrigation
- Paramparagat Krishi Vikas Yojana organic farming
- Rainfed Area Development
- Agroforestry
- Crop Diversification Programme
- Agri-mechanisation and Crop Residue Management
- Agri Startup and Innovation support

So a farmer applying for a drip system, a soil test, an organic cluster or a stubble management machine is often applying under this umbrella, under the state's own scheme name.

**Who qualifies**
Farmers qualify through the individual component. Eligibility follows the component's rules.
Farmer Producer Organisations, SHGs, agri start-ups and rural entrepreneurs also access RKVY-funded projects.

**How to apply**
1. Ask the block agriculture officer or the district agriculture officer which RKVY components are running in the district this year.
2. Apply through the state agriculture department portal or the district office.
3. Follow the component's own document list and verification process.

**Deadline pattern**
⚠ Components, names and rates are decided by each state each financial year. Nothing here is fixed nationally. Always confirm the current year's state action plan at the block office.

## 12. National Mission on Edible Oils (NMEO)

The mission runs in two parts.

### 12a. NMEO-Oilseeds
Approved for 2024-25 to 2030-31 with an outlay of Rupees 10,103 crore.
Focus crops: rapeseed-mustard, groundnut, soybean, sunflower, sesamum, safflower, niger, linseed and castor.
Also covers cottonseed, rice bran, coconut and tree-borne oilseeds.

**What the farmer gets**
- High quality certified seed of improved varieties, supplied through the SATHI seed traceability portal
- Cluster-based Value Chain Partnerships, where 65 oilseed clusters are developed with FPOs, cooperatives and industry
- Training on Good Agricultural Practices
- Advisory on weather, sowing window and pest management
- Support for intercropping oilseeds and for growing oilseeds on rice fallow land
- Assured procurement backing through the Minimum Support Price for oilseed crops

**How to apply**
Approach the district or block agriculture office, the KVK, or the FPO working in an oilseed cluster.
Seed is booked through the SATHI portal at seednet.gov.in or through the state seed corporation.

### 12b. NMEO-Oil Palm
Support for planting material, maintenance during the gestation years, drip irrigation, borewells and harvesting tools for oil palm growers.
A price assurance formula protects the grower on fresh fruit bunch prices.
Approach the state horticulture department or the assigned oil palm processing company.

⚠ Component rates, cluster locations and the assistance table are notified state by state each year.

## 13. Seed Schemes and Seed Subsidy

**What the farmer gets**
- Subsidy on certified seed of notified varieties, given through the National Food Security Mission and other crop missions. The subsidy is a fixed amount per quintal, or a percentage of the seed cost.
- Seed Village Programme: foundation and certified seed is supplied at subsidised cost to a group of farmers in a village, along with training in seed production and seed treatment. Support is also given for seed storage bins.
- Seed minikits of new and high-yielding varieties, distributed free during campaign drives, especially for pulses and oilseeds.

## 16. Integrated Pest Management (IPM) — the official approach

**What IPM means**
IPM is the official crop protection strategy of the Directorate of Plant Protection, Quarantine and Storage and the National Institute of Plant Health Management.
IPM brings together several ways of managing a pest — cultural, physical, mechanical, biological, botanical and chemical.
Chemical pesticide is kept as the last step, used only when the other steps have been tried and the pest is still causing economic damage.

**The order to work in**

1. **Start with a healthy crop.**
   Choose a resistant or tolerant variety recommended for the district.
   Use certified, healthy seed and treat it before sowing.
   Sow at the recommended time and spacing.
   Keep the soil healthy with organic matter and balanced nutrition.

2. **Use cultural practices.**
   Rotate crops so a pest loses its host.
   Practise summer deep ploughing to expose resting stages of pests to the sun and to birds.
   Keep field bunds clean of weed hosts.
   Grow trap crops and border crops as recommended for the main crop.
   Grow flowering plants on the bund to feed natural enemies.
   Destroy crop stubble that harbours a pest carry-over.

3. **Watch the field every week — AESA.**
   The official method is Agro-Ecosystem Analysis. Walk the field weekly.
   Count the pests. Also count the defenders — spiders, ladybird beetles, dragonflies, wasps, predatory bugs.
   Look at the plant's own condition and its ability to recover.
   Note the weather, the soil moisture and the crop stage.
   Draw the field picture and decide as a group at the Farmer Field School.

4. **Use physical and mechanical steps.**
   Hand-pick and destroy egg masses, larvae and infested shoots.
   Install pheromone traps to monitor and mass-trap moths.
   Install light traps, yellow sticky traps and blue sticky traps as recommended.
   Put up bird perches so birds pick larvae.
   Rogue out virus-infected plants early.

5. **Use biological control.**
   Release bio-control agents such as Trichogramma, Chrysoperla and predatory mites where recommended.
   Use bio-pesticides — Trichoderma, Pseudomonas, Bacillus thuringiensis, Beauveria, Metarhizium, NPV.
   Use neem-based botanical formulations.
   Protect the natural enemies already in the field by holding back on broad-spectrum sprays.

6. **Use chemical pesticide last.**
   Spray only when the pest crosses the Economic Threshold Level for that crop and pest.
   Choose a pesticide approved by the Central Insecticides Board and Registration Committee for that crop and that pest.
   Choose the least hazardous product that will do the job.
   Rotate chemical groups across sprays so the pest does not build resistance.
   Spot-spray the affected patch instead of the whole field where the pest is localised.

**Key idea to remember**
A pest present in the field is not by itself a reason to spray.
Spraying is justified when the pest population crosses the threshold at which the damage costs more than the control.
Every spray that goes out before that point also kills the defenders that were working for free.

## 17. Safe Pesticide Handling — official Do's and Don'ts

Source of this guidance: Directorate of Plant Protection, Quarantine and Storage, "Instruction for Safe Use of Pesticide".

### Buying

Do:
- Buy from a licensed, registered pesticide dealer.
- Ask for a proper cash memo with the product name, batch number and date.
- Check that the container is sealed and the label is intact and readable.
- Check the expiry date on the label.
- Buy only the quantity needed for the immediate operation.
- Check that the product is approved for that crop and that pest.

The safest habit: read the whole label before buying, and buy for the job at hand.

### Storing

Do:
- Keep pesticides in a separate store away from the house, away from the kitchen and away from the cattle shed.
- Keep the product in its own original container with the label on it.
- Keep the store locked, with children and animals well away.
- Keep weedicides on a separate shelf from insecticides and fungicides.
- Put a warning sign on the store.
- Protect stock from direct sunlight and from rain.

Keeping the product in its original labelled container is the single most important storage rule. It is what tells a doctor what to treat, if anything goes wrong.

### Mixing the spray solution

Do:
- Wear full protective gear — hand gloves, face mask, cap, apron or full-sleeve cotton clothing, full trousers and covered footwear.
- Use clean water.
- Read the label and measure the recommended quantity with a separate marked measure.
- Mix in the open air, standing so the wind carries the fumes away.
- Use a stick to stir, and keep hands out of the solution.
- Prepare only the quantity needed for that day and use it the same day.
- Keep drinking water, soap and a change of clothes nearby.

Keep the mouth, eyes and skin clear of the concentrate. Keep food, drink, tobacco and bidis outside the mixing area.

### Spraying

Do:
- Spray on a calm, cool day. Early morning and late afternoon are the good windows.
- Stand so the wind carries the spray away from the body, and walk backwards out of the sprayed area.
- Check the sprayer for leaks before filling it.
- Clear the nozzle with a soft brush or by washing it, keeping it away from the mouth.
- Put up a warning sign on a freshly sprayed field.
- Take a break and step into fresh air if the head feels heavy.
- Keep other people, children and grazing animals away from the field during and just after spraying.

Choose a calm day, keep the wind at the back, and stay out of the drift.

### After spraying

Do:
- Wash the sprayer, the nozzles and the measuring vessels in an isolated spot away from wells, ponds, canals and drinking water sources.
- Pour leftover spray solution into a pit dug in an isolated waste area, well away from any water body.
- Take a full bath with soap and put on clean clothes.
- Wash the sprayed clothes separately from family clothes.
- Wash hands and face thoroughly before eating, drinking or smoking.
- Note the date of spraying in a field diary, so the pre-harvest interval can be counted.

### Empty container disposal

Official practice:
1. Rinse the empty container three times with clean water. Pour each rinse into the spray tank and use it on the crop.
2. Puncture and crush the empty container so it can never be used again.
3. Bury the crushed container in a deep pit dug in a waste area, away from any water source and away from grazing land.
4. Keep the empty container out of the house entirely.

An empty pesticide container is treated as unusable for anything else. It is not used for storing water, grain, oil, milk or feed, and it is not sold to a scrap dealer for reuse.

## 18. The Pesticide Toxicity Triangle — colour code on the label

Every registered pesticide container in India carries a coloured triangle on the label. The colour tells the toxicity class at a glance, without reading a word.

**Bright RED triangle — Extremely toxic**
Word on the label: POISON, with a skull and crossbones.
Oral LD50 of 1 to 50 mg per kg. Dermal LD50 of 1 to 200 mg per kg.
This is the most hazardous class. Handle with full protective gear, and treat any exposure as an emergency.

**Bright YELLOW triangle — Highly toxic**
Word on the label: POISON.
Oral LD50 of 51 to 500 mg per kg. Dermal LD50 of 201 to 2000 mg per kg.
Still very hazardous. Full protective gear applies.

**Bright BLUE triangle — Moderately toxic**
Word on the label: DANGER.
Oral LD50 of 501 to 5000 mg per kg. Dermal LD50 of 2001 to 20000 mg per kg.

**Bright GREEN triangle — Slightly toxic**
Word on the label: CAUTION.
Oral LD50 above 5000 mg per kg. Dermal LD50 above 20000 mg per kg.
This is the least hazardous class registered for use.

**How to use the colour**
Look at the triangle before buying and again before opening.
Prefer the green or blue class where an equally effective option exists for that pest.
Full protective gear applies to every colour. The colour tells how serious a mistake would be, and every class calls for care.

Also read on the label: the CIB&RC registration number, the crop and pest the product is approved for, the recommended dose, the waiting period before harvest, the antidote statement, and the expiry date.

## 19. Pesticide Poisoning — first response

**Signs to act on**
Headache, dizziness, heavy sweating, excess saliva, watering eyes, blurred vision, pinpoint pupils, nausea, vomiting, stomach cramps, muscle twitching, weakness, difficulty breathing, confusion, fits.
Any of these appearing during or after handling pesticide calls for immediate action.

**Steps, in order**

1. **Move the person to fresh air, away from the pesticide.**
   Get the person out of the sprayed field or the store immediately.

2. **Remove contaminated clothing.**
   Take off all clothes that have pesticide on them. Wrap the person in something clean.

3. **Wash the skin.**
   Wash the affected skin, hair and nails with plenty of clean water and soap. Keep washing for at least 15 minutes.

4. **Rinse the eyes if the eyes are affected.**
   Hold the eyelids open and rinse with clean running water for 15 minutes.

5. **Keep the person calm, resting and warm.**
   Loosen tight clothing at the neck and waist. Turn an unconscious person on their side so the airway stays clear.

6. **Call for help and get the person to a hospital.**
   Call the ambulance on **108**.
   Call the National Poisons Information Centre at AIIMS New Delhi, toll free, 24 hours a day: **1800 116 117**.

7. **Carry the pesticide container or the label to the hospital.**
   This is the most useful thing anyone can do. The doctor needs to know the exact chemical to choose the right antidote.
   Carry the empty container, or the label, or a clear photo of the label.

8. **Tell the doctor everything.**
   Say what was used, how much, how long ago, and by what route — skin, breathing or swallowing.

**Things that help**
- Keep the person awake and lying on their side.
- Keep the airway clear.
- Carry the label.

**Things to leave to the doctor**
- Giving milk, oil or any home remedy by mouth.
- Making the person vomit.
- Giving anything by mouth to a person who is drowsy, unconscious or having fits.

The National Poisons Information Centre runs 24 hours from the Department of Pharmacology, AIIMS New Delhi, and answers calls from the public and from doctors.

## 20. Pre-Harvest Interval and Residue Limits

**What the pre-harvest interval is**
The pre-harvest interval, also called the waiting period, is the number of days that must pass between the last pesticide spray and harvest.
It is printed on the pesticide label for each crop.
Counting those days lets the residue break down to a safe level before the produce is picked.

**What a Maximum Residue Limit is**
The Maximum Residue Limit, or MRL, is the highest amount of a pesticide residue legally allowed on a food commodity.
MRLs in India are notified by the Food Safety and Standards Authority of India under the Food Safety and Standards Regulations.
The limit is worked out from supervised field trials, where the pesticide is applied at the approved dose and the residue is measured over time.

**Why the waiting period matters to the farmer's income**
- Produce within the MRL passes food safety testing and moves freely in the mandi and in export channels.
- Buyers, exporters and organised retail chains test consignments. A clean residue report keeps the buyer coming back.
- Consignments meeting residue norms qualify for export and fetch a better price.
- Following the label dose and the waiting period is what Good Agricultural Practice means in official terms.

**How to get it right**
1. Read the waiting period from the label before spraying.
2. Write the spray date in a field diary or on the calendar in the house.
3. Count forward from that date to the earliest harvest date.
4. Harvest on or after that date.
5. Plan sprays backwards from the expected picking date, especially for vegetables and fruit that are picked repeatedly.

**Crops that need extra attention**
Vegetables and fruits harvested every few days — bhindi, brinjal, chilli, tomato, cucurbits, grapes, beans — carry the highest residue risk, because the picking interval can be shorter than the waiting period.
For these, choose products with a short waiting period, prefer bio-pesticides and neem formulations close to harvest, and stagger picking blocks so a sprayed block is left to complete its waiting period.

⚠ The waiting period differs for every pesticide and every crop. Take it from the label of the actual product in hand.

Sources: https://fssai.gov.in/upload/uploadfiles/files/Guidance_Document_SOP_MRL_16_03_2022.pdf | https://fssai.gov.in/upload/uploadfiles/files/Draft_Guidance_Document_SOP_Limit_Pesticides_09_04_2021.pdf | https://ppqs.gov.in/divisions/cib-rc/major-uses-of-pesticides

## 21. Soil Testing — sampling, sending and reading the card

### When to take a sample
Take the sample after harvest, when the field carries no standing crop.
The usual times are after the Rabi harvest and after the Kharif harvest.
Take it before the next crop's fertiliser and manure go in.
Test the same field once every 2 to 3 years, or every year for intensively cropped and irrigated land.

### How to draw the sample

1. **Divide the farm into uniform blocks.**
   Treat each block separately where the soil colour, slope, past cropping or irrigation differs.
   One composite sample represents one uniform block.

2. **Choose 8 to 10 spots** spread across the block, walking a zigzag path.

3. **Clear the surface** at each spot. Scrape away grass, stubble, pebbles and fresh manure from the top.

4. **Cut a V-shaped slice.**
   Dig a V-shaped pit to the required depth. Then shave a uniform 2 to 3 cm thick slice from top to bottom of one face of the V.
   Depth for field crops: 0 to 15 cm, the plough layer.
   Depth for orchards and plantation crops: take separate samples for 0 to 30 cm, 30 to 60 cm and 60 to 90 cm.

5. **Collect all slices in one clean bucket** or on a clean cloth or polythene sheet.

6. **Mix thoroughly.**

7. **Reduce by quartering.**
   Spread the mixed soil into a circle. Divide it into four quarters. Throw away two opposite quarters. Mix the remaining two. Repeat until about half a kilogram of soil is left.

8. **Bag and label.**
   Put the soil in a clean cloth or polythene bag.
   Write on the label: farmer's name, village, block, district, survey or khasra number, field identification, sampling depth, date, the previous crop grown, the next crop planned, and whether the land is irrigated or rainfed.
   Put one label inside the bag and tie one outside.

### Where to avoid drawing the sample
Draw the sample away from field bunds and boundaries, away from the old manure heap site, away from trees and shade, away from the irrigation channel, away from marshy and waterlogged spots, away from where the compost or fertiliser was last dumped, and away from the cattle standing area. These spots do not represent the field.

### Where to send it
- The village level soil testing lab, where the state has set one up
- The block or district Soil Testing Laboratory of the state agriculture department
- The nearest Krishi Vigyan Kendra
- The State Agricultural University soil science laboratory
- A mobile soil testing van when it visits the village

Register the sample on soilhealth.dac.gov.in, or let the lab register it. Keep the sample registration number and the mobile number handy to collect the report.

### How to read a Soil Health Card

The card lists 12 values with a rating for each.

**pH — how acid or alkaline the soil is.**
Around 6.5 to 7.5 is the comfortable range for most crops.
A value below about 6.0 means acidic soil. Liming is the usual correction.
A value above about 8.0 means alkaline or sodic soil. Gypsum and organic matter are the usual correction.
pH matters because it decides how much of the nutrient present in the soil the plant can actually take up.

**EC — Electrical Conductivity, the salt level.**
A low value means normal soil. A high value means salt is building up and will stress the crop.

**OC — Organic Carbon, the life of the soil.**
Higher is better. Low organic carbon means the soil needs farmyard manure, compost, green manure and crop residue incorporation.
Organic carbon drives water holding, structure and nutrient release.

**N, P, K — the main nutrients.**
Each is rated Low, Medium or High.
Low means the crop will need the full recommended dose or more.
Medium means the standard recommended dose.
High means the dose can be reduced for that nutrient.

**S — Sulphur.** Important for oilseeds, pulses and mustard.

**Zn, Fe, Cu, Mn, B — micronutrients.**
Each is rated Sufficient or Deficient.
A deficient rating calls for a specific micronutrient application, often as a soil dose at sowing or a foliar spray at the recommended crop stage.

**The recommendation block**
The card carries a crop-wise fertiliser recommendation table.
It gives the quantity of fertiliser per acre or per hectare for the crops the farmer named.
It usually gives two options — a straight fertiliser combination and an option including organic manure.

**What to do with the card**
1. Take the card to the fertiliser shop and buy to the recommendation, not to habit.
2. Correct pH and organic carbon first. Nutrient response improves once those are right.
3. Apply the micronutrient the card marks as deficient. This is often where the biggest yield gain sits.
4. Show the card to the KVK scientist or the agriculture officer if any rating is unclear.
5. Keep the card and compare the next test in 2 to 3 years, to see whether organic carbon is climbing.

## 22. Seed Treatment Basics

**Why treat seed**
Seed treatment protects the seed and the young seedling from soil-borne and seed-borne disease and from early insect attack.
It is the cheapest plant protection step in the whole season, since a small quantity of material protects the whole crop at its most vulnerable stage.
It also improves germination and gives an even, vigorous stand.

**The correct order — three steps, in this sequence**

1. **Fungicide first.**
   Treat with the recommended seed dressing fungicide for that crop.
   This handles seed-borne fungal diseases, smuts, seedling blight and damping off.

2. **Insecticide second.**
   Treat with the recommended seed treatment insecticide where early sucking pest or termite attack is expected.

3. **Biofertiliser and bio-agent last, just before sowing.**
   Treat with Rhizobium for pulses and groundnut, Azospirillum or Azotobacter for cereals, and phosphate solubilising bacteria for most crops.
   Bio-agents such as Trichoderma and Pseudomonas also go at this stage.

Keep a gap between each step, and dry the seed in the shade after each one.
Biological cultures go on last and go on close to sowing, because live organisms survive better when they meet the soil soon.

**Where a bio-agent is the whole treatment**
Where the farmer chooses a fully biological route, Trichoderma or Pseudomonas alone is used as the seed treatment. This is the standard route for organic and natural farming, and it keeps the seed compatible with the Rhizobium culture that follows.

**How to treat the seed — the practical method**

1. Work in the shade, on a clean cloth or polythene sheet or in a clean drum.
2. Wear gloves and a mask.
3. Measure the seed. Measure the treatment material per the label rate for that weight of seed.
4. Sprinkle a little clean water or a sticker such as rice gruel, jaggery solution or gum, so the material holds on the seed.
5. Add the treatment material and mix until every seed carries an even coat. A hand-operated seed treating drum does this best.
6. Spread the seed in the shade and let it dry.
7. Sow the same day where the treatment is biological. Fungicide-treated seed can be held a short while as the label allows.

**Safety points**
- Treat seed away from the kitchen, food grain and drinking water.
- Wear gloves, a mask and full clothing. Wash hands and face with soap afterwards.
- Keep treated seed clearly marked and out of reach of children and animals.
- Treated seed is planting material only. Keep it entirely separate from grain meant for eating or for feed.
- Sow all the treated seed. Bury any leftover treated seed deep in a waste area.
- Empty treatment packets are crushed and buried, like pesticide containers.

⚠ Products, rates and compatibility differ by crop and by state recommendation. Take the current recommendation from the KVK, the state agriculture department package of practices, or the Kisan Call Centre.

## 23. Krishi Vigyan Kendra (KVK) — when and how to approach

**What a KVK is**
A Krishi Vigyan Kendra is the district level farm science centre of the Indian Council of Agricultural Research.
There are 731 KVKs covering 638 rural districts. The first one was set up in Puducherry in 1974.
A KVK is hosted by a State Agricultural University, an ICAR institute, a state department or a registered NGO, and staffed by subject matter specialists in agronomy, plant protection, horticulture, soil science, animal science, home science and agricultural engineering.

**What a KVK does for the farmer**
- Diagnoses crop problems. Bring an affected plant, and get an identification and an advisory.
- Runs a soil, water and plant testing service.
- Runs on-farm demonstrations of new varieties and practices, on farmers' own fields.
- Runs skill training — nursery raising, grafting, seed production, mushroom growing, beekeeping, vermicompost, dairy management, food processing, farm machinery operation.
- Produces and supplies quality seed, planting material, bio-fertiliser and bio-agent cultures.
- Issues local agro-advisories on weather, sowing windows and pest outbreaks, including through the Kisan Sarathi platform.
- Runs Farmer Field Schools and field days.
- Guides farmers into FPOs and into value addition and marketing.

**Good reasons to visit a KVK**
- A crop problem the farmer cannot identify — bring a whole affected plant with roots, and photos of the field.
- Choosing a variety for the coming season.
- Getting soil tested and getting the recommendation explained.
- Learning a new enterprise — mushroom, apiary, poultry, goat rearing, vermicompost, processing.
- Getting a fair second opinion on a dealer's spray recommendation.
- Starting organic or natural farming.
- Understanding which schemes are running in the district this year.
- Getting genuine seed and planting material of a new variety.

**How to find and approach a KVK**
1. Find the district KVK on kvk.icar.gov.in. The portal lists every KVK with address, phone number and staff.
2. Phone ahead and ask which day the subject specialist is available. Many KVKs keep a fixed farmers' day each week.
3. Carry the affected plant sample, a soil sample if a test is wanted, the land record and Aadhaar.
4. Register on Kisan Sarathi (kisansarathi.icar.gov.in) to receive advisories on the mobile.
5. Ask to be added to the KVK's farmer group so training announcements reach the phone.

Most KVK advisory and diagnostic services are free. Seed, planting material and some trainings carry a small cost.

Sources: https://kvk.icar.gov.in/ | https://icar.org.in/en/krishi-vigyan-kendras-kvks | https://icar.org.in/en/agricultural-extension-division/krishi-vigyan-kendra-kvk-farm-science-centre | https://agriculture.vikaspedia.in/viewcontent/agriculture/agri-directory/krishi-vigyan-kendras | https://kvk.icar.gov.in/download/Contact_Details.pdf

- Advise on post-harvest handling and storage.

**How to get the most out of a call**
1. Keep the crop name, variety, sowing date, field area and irrigation type ready.
2. Describe the symptom concretely — which part of the plant, what colour, how much of the field, since how many days.
3. Say the district and block, so the advice fits the local package of practices.
4. Note down the answer, and note the ticket or query number.
5. Register the mobile number on mkisan.gov.in to receive free advisory SMS on crops of interest.

Every call is recorded and logged. An unanswered query is escalated and returned.

## 25. Minimum Support Price (MSP) — the basics

**What MSP is**
The Minimum Support Price is a price the Government announces before the sowing season.
It is the price at which government agencies will buy the notified crop from farmers during the procurement season.
Knowing the price before sowing lets the farmer plan the crop.

**How many crops**
MSP is announced for 22 mandated crops every year.

**The 22 crops**

Kharif — 14 crops:
Paddy (Common and Grade A), jowar (Hybrid and Maldandi), bajra, maize, ragi, arhar or tur, moong, urad, groundnut, soybean, sunflower seed, sesamum, nigerseed, cotton (medium staple and long staple).

Rabi — 6 crops:
Wheat, barley, gram, masur or lentil, rapeseed and mustard, safflower.

Commercial — 2 crops:
Jute, and copra (milling copra and ball copra).

Fair and Remunerative Price (FRP) for sugarcane is announced separately, and mills pay it to cane growers.

**Who decides the price**
The Commission for Agricultural Costs and Prices (CACP) recommends the price.
CACP looks at the cost of production, demand and supply in India and abroad, domestic and international prices, price parity between crops, terms of trade between agriculture and other sectors, and the likely effect on the wider economy.
Since 2018-19 the policy is to fix MSP at a level giving a margin of at least 50 percent over the cost of production.
The Government announces the final price after taking views from State Governments and concerned Central Ministries.

**How a farmer actually sells at MSP**

1. **Watch for the state procurement notification.** It names the procurement centres, the opening and closing dates, and the quality specifications.
2. **Register on the state procurement portal** before the season. Registration usually needs Aadhaar, the land record showing the sown area, and the Aadhaar-linked bank account.
3. **Get a token or slot.** Most states issue a date and a centre so the arrival is staggered.
4. **Prepare the produce to the notified quality specification** — moisture level, foreign matter, damaged and discoloured grain, and shrivelled and broken grain all have limits. Clean, dry and grade the produce before bringing it.
5. **Bring the produce to the procurement centre** on the token date, with the registration slip, Aadhaar and the bank passbook.
6. **The produce is weighed and quality checked.** A receipt is issued.
7. **Payment comes into the Aadhaar-linked bank account** by direct benefit transfer, within the timeline the state sets.

**Points worth knowing**
- Selling at MSP is a choice. A farmer may sell to a government agency at MSP or in the open market, whichever pays better.
- Paddy and wheat procurement runs widely, through the Food Corporation of India and state agencies.
- Pulses, oilseeds and copra are procured under the Price Support Scheme through NAFED and NCCF when the market price falls below MSP.
- Cotton is procured by the Cotton Corporation of India.
- Meeting the notified quality specification is what secures the MSP rate. Cleaning and drying before arrival is the highest-return work a farmer can do at that point.

⚠ MSP rates are announced fresh for each Kharif and Rabi season. Procurement centres, registration portals, dates and quality norms are notified state by state. Get the current rate from the state agriculture department, the mandi board, agmarknet.gov.in, or by calling 1800-180-1551.

## 26. Crop Residue and Stubble Management

**The official position**
Government guidance is to keep crop residue in the field or put it to use, rather than burn it.
Burning destroys the organic carbon, nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium and sulphur held in the straw. It kills soil microbes and earthworms in the top layer. It adds to air pollution.
The Crop Residue Management scheme supports both in-situ and ex-situ management with machinery subsidy and project support.

**Route 1 — In-situ management. Keep the residue in the field.**

Machines supported under the scheme:
- **Super Straw Management System (Super SMS)** fitted to the combine harvester. It chops and spreads the straw evenly while harvesting, instead of leaving a windrow.
- **Happy Seeder.** Sows wheat directly into standing paddy stubble, cutting and lifting the straw and dropping it back as mulch over the sown row.
- **Super Seeder.** Cuts, lifts and mixes the straw into the soil and sows in one pass.
- **Smart Seeder** and **Surface Seeder.** Lighter options for sowing into residue.
- **Zero Till Seed cum Fertiliser Drill.** Sows into the untilled field.
- **Mulcher, Shredder and Rotary Slasher.** Chop the residue fine and spread it.
- **Reversible Mould Board Plough.** Turns residue into the soil.
- **Crop Reaper and Reaper Binder.** Cuts and bundles the straw for removal.

What in-situ management gives back:
- Organic carbon builds in the soil year after year.
- The nutrients held in the straw return to the field.
- Mulch holds soil moisture and cuts the first irrigation requirement.
- Weed pressure falls under a straw mulch.
- Soil temperature stays steadier for the young crop.
- Sowing the next crop happens sooner, since the field needs no clearing.

**Route 2 — Ex-situ management. Take the residue out and use it.**

Collection machines: Baler and Straw Rake. These gather the loose straw into bales that can be lifted and transported.

Uses for collected straw:
- Fuel pellets and briquettes for thermal power plants and industrial boilers
- Compressed Bio-Gas (CBG) and bio-CNG plants
- Biogas
- Ethanol and alcohol production
- Cattle fodder, after treatment
- Composting into farmyard-quality manure
- Mushroom growing substrate
- Packaging board, paper and biodegradable products
- Farm bedding

The revised guidelines allow techno-commercial pilot projects where a farmer or FPO signs a bilateral agreement with an industry that will take the straw, and biomass collection depots are set up to aggregate it.

**Financial support**
- 50 percent of machine cost to an individual farmer for crop residue management machinery.
- 80 percent of project cost for projects up to Rupees 30 lakh, for rural entrepreneurs, cooperative societies, registered farmer societies, FPOs and panchayats setting up a Custom Hiring Centre.
- A farmer who does not want to buy can hire the machine from a Custom Hiring Centre. Find nearby machines on the FARMS app or through the CHC portal.

**Practical sequence for a paddy-wheat farmer**
1. Fit or hire a combine harvester with a Super SMS attached, so the straw comes out chopped and spread.
2. Choose the sowing route — Happy Seeder or Super Seeder to sow wheat directly into the residue.
3. Book the machine from the Custom Hiring Centre before the harvest window opens, since demand peaks in a narrow period.
4. Where the straw is to be removed, book a baler and arrange the buyer or depot in advance.
5. Give the first irrigation a little later than usual, since the mulch holds moisture.

⚠ The Crop Residue Management scheme is focused on Punjab, Haryana, Uttar Pradesh, Madhya Pradesh and NCT of Delhi. Other states support the same machinery through SMAM and the state agriculture department, under different names and rates.
