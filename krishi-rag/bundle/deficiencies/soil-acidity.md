# What acidic soil does to a crop

Acidity is not a disease and produces no lesion to point at. It shows up as a
crop that underperforms for no visible reason:

- Fertiliser gives less response than it should — **phosphorus in particular
  gets locked up** and becomes unavailable however much is applied.
- Aluminium and manganese become soluble at low pH and **damage roots**, so
  plants stay small with poor root systems.
- Calcium and magnesium are scarce.
- Soil biological activity falls, so organic matter breaks down more slowly and
  nutrient release slows with it.

The practical signature is a field where **inputs are applied correctly and the
crop still disappoints**, in the same patches every season.

# Why this cannot be answered without a test

Lime requirement depends on the soil's actual pH, its texture, and its organic
matter — the same field can need different amounts across it, and applying lime
to a soil that is not acidic **causes harm** by pushing pH the other way and
locking up micronutrients such as zinc and iron.

**A lime recommendation for a pH 5.2 acid alfisol is actively wrong advice on
an alkaline soil.** There is no national answer to this question.

**This device does not carry verified lime rates.** The correct action is a
soil test, then a recommendation from a Krishi Vigyan Kendra or soil testing
laboratory for that specific field.

# Where this applies

Jharkhand, Odisha, West Bengal, Assam, the north-eastern states, and parts of
Chhattisgarh and Bihar — acid alfisols and red lateritic soils. Long-term
experiments at Birsa Agricultural University, Ranchi, running to sixty years,
document how fertiliser, manure and lime interact on these soils and how
acidity limits crop response.

Anything stated here about acid soils **does not transfer** to the alluvial
soils of the north-western plains or the black cotton soils of the Deccan,
which have the opposite problem.
