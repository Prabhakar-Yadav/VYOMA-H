### What kind of organism, and what that means
***Xanthomonas* is a BACTERIUM** — aerobic, Gram-negative, non-spore-forming, rod-shaped,
1–2 × 0.8–1.0 µm, with a single polar flagellum (monotrichous). Colonies are circular, convex,
whitish-yellow to straw-yellow and opaque; the yellow pigment (xanthomonadin) gives the genus its
name.
(eagri PATH 272 Lecture 01 — http://eagri.org/eagri50/PATH272/lecture01/0011.html)

**Because it is a bacterium, management is built on cultural practice, clean planting material and
resistant varieties.** Bacteria multiply in water films and in plant sap, enter only through
natural openings and wounds, and are moved by water and by hands and tools. The actions that work
are therefore: **clean seed and setts, resistant or pyramided-resistance varieties, field
sanitation and stubble destruction, drainage and avoidance of flooding, avoidance of wounding,
disinfection of knives and secateurs, removal of weed and collateral hosts, balanced nitrogen, and
roguing.** Copper-based protectants and antibiotic-class materials are used as surface protectants
and to slow spread; the durable control comes from the cultural and genetic measures.

### Bacterial leaf blight of rice — the reference case

**Symptoms.** Usually noticed at heading, but it can occur earlier. Seedlings in the nursery show
circular yellow spots at the margin that enlarge and coalesce, drying the foliage. The **"kresek"
symptom** appears in seedlings **1–2 weeks after transplanting**: the bacteria **enter through the
cut wounds in the clipped leaf tips**, become systemic, and kill the entire seedling. In grown
plants, **water-soaked, translucent lesions appear near the leaf margin**, enlarge in length and
width with a **wavy margin**, and **turn straw-yellow within a few days**, eventually covering the
lamina, which turns white or straw-coloured. **Milky or opaque dew drops containing bacterial
masses form on young lesions in the early morning** and dry to a white encrustation.
(eagri PATH 272 Lecture 01 — http://eagri.org/eagri50/PATH272/lecture01/0011.html)

**The field diagnostic — the ooze test.** *"If the cut end of the leaf is dipped in water, it
becomes turbid because of bacterial ooze."* This costs nothing, needs only a glass of clear water,
and distinguishes a bacterial disease from a fungal one in about a minute. **This single test is
one of the highest-value items a farmer advisory can teach.**
(eagri PATH 272 Lecture 01)

**Favourable conditions.**
- **Clipping the tips of seedlings at transplanting** — this creates the wound the bacterium needs.
- **Heavy rain, heavy dew, flooding, deep irrigation water.**
- **Severe wind** (wind-driven rain wounds leaves and moves bacteria) and **temperature of
  25–30 °C**.
- **Excessive nitrogen, especially late top-dressing.**
(eagri PATH 272 Lecture 01 — http://eagri.org/eagri50/PATH272/lecture01/0011.html)

**Disease cycle and survival.** The pathogen **survives in soil, in infected stubble, and on
collateral hosts *Leersia* spp., *Plantago major*, *Paspalum distichum* and *Cynodon dactylon*
(bermuda/hariali grass)**. It **spreads through irrigation water and through rain storms**.
Infected seed is a comparatively minor source, because the bacterial numbers fall rapidly and the
bacteria die during seed soaking.
(eagri PATH 272 Lecture 01)

**How it enters the plant.** Through **hydathodes at the leaf margin and tip** (the natural water
pores from which guttation drops form — which is why the disease starts at the margin and why
guttation-favouring humid nights promote it), and through **wounds**, especially clipped leaf tips
and wind-whipped leaves. It then colonises the **xylem** and moves systemically, which is why
kresek kills the whole seedling.

**Incubation period.** Symptoms appear roughly **7–14 days** after infection under favourable
conditions; kresek shows within **1–2 weeks of transplanting**. ⚠

**Management (positively stated).**
- **Burn the stubble** to remove the surviving population.
- **Use an optimum dose of fertiliser**; split nitrogen and keep late top-dressing moderate.
- **Transplant without clipping the seedling tips** — retain intact leaf tips so the bacterium has
  no wound to enter.
- **Maintain shallow, intermittent irrigation with good drainage**; keep water from an affected
  field out of a healthy field.
- **Remove weed and collateral hosts** from bunds and channels.
- **Grow resistant cultivars** — TNAU lists IR 20 and TKM 6; modern practice deploys **pyramided
  resistance combining Xa21 + xa13 + xa5**, which is far more durable than any single gene.
(eagri PATH 272 Lecture 01 — http://eagri.org/eagri50/PATH272/lecture01/0011.html)

**Why resistance breaks down.** *X. oryzae* pv. *oryzae* populations in India are divided into
**pathotypes** distinguished by which Xa genes they overcome. The bacterium uses **TAL effectors**
that bind specific promoter sequences in the rice genome to switch on susceptibility genes; a small
change in the effector, or in the plant's promoter, changes the recognition. When one Xa gene is
deployed across a large area, pathotypes that escape it are strongly selected. **Pyramiding
several Xa genes into one variety requires the pathogen to change several things at once and is
therefore far more durable.** ⚠ Pathotype frequencies differ markedly between Indian states and
shift over years.

### Bacterial leaf streak of rice — *X. oryzae* pv. *oryzicola*
Fine **translucent streaks form BETWEEN the veins** (interveinal), enlarge lengthwise, turn brown,
and under humid conditions **bacterial ooze forms small yellow band-like exudates** on the lesion
surface. **The diagnostic contrast with BLB: streak lesions are interveinal, linear and
translucent when held to the light, and begin anywhere on the blade; blight lesions begin at the
leaf margin or tip and have a wavy margin.** *X. o.* pv. *oryzicola* enters mainly through
**stomata** rather than hydathodes, which is why it produces streaks in the leaf blade rather than
marginal blight. Management is the same package: burn stubble, optimum fertiliser, avoid clipping
seedling tips, avoid flooded conditions, remove weed hosts, grow resistant cultivars.
(eagri PATH 272 Lecture 01 — http://eagri.org/eagri50/PATH272/lecture01/0012.html)

### Citrus canker — *X. citri* subsp. *citri*
Raised, corky, **crater-like lesions with a water-soaked yellow halo** on leaves, twigs and fruit;
lesions are visible on **both leaf surfaces**, which separates canker from scab. It enters through
**stomata and through wounds**, and **wounds made by the citrus leaf miner (*Phyllocnistis
citrella*) dramatically increase infection** — so **leaf miner management is a canker management
measure**. Spread is by **wind-driven rain**, which is the dominant dispersal route; **the practical
implication is that windbreaks reduce canker**, and that operations should be avoided when the
canopy is wet. Survives in **lesions on twigs and in leaf litter**. Managed by clean nursery
material, pruning and burning of cankered twigs, windbreaks, leaf miner management, copper
protectants, and tolerant rootstock/scion combinations. ⚠

### Black rot of crucifers — *X. campestris* pv. *campestris*
The textbook **seed-borne, hydathode-entering** bacterium. Characteristic **V-shaped yellow lesions
opening from the leaf margin inward, with blackened veins** inside the V. It enters through
**hydathodes at the leaf margin**, colonises the **xylem**, and blackens the vascular bundles —
cut the petiole and the black ring of vessels is diagnostic. **Seed-borne**, surviving on and in
seed for **2–3 years**, and surviving in **infected crucifer debris in soil for about 1–2 years**
(it survives as long as the host debris persists, and declines rapidly once the debris decomposes).
Managed by **hot-water seed treatment (about 50 °C for 20–30 minutes — a treatment that works
because the bacterium is killed at a temperature the seed tolerates)**, certified clean seed,
**3-year rotation out of crucifers**, removal of cruciferous weeds (wild mustard, shepherd's
purse), avoidance of overhead irrigation, and resistant varieties. ⚠ Hot-water temperatures and
times differ by crucifer species; the treatment must be precise.

### Pomegranate bacterial blight ("telya") — *X. axonopodis* pv. *punicae*
Water-soaked, **oily-looking dark spots** on leaves and fruit, with **fruit cracking along the
lesion**, which destroys market value. Enters through **stomata and wounds**; spreads by
**rain splash, wind-driven rain, and contaminated pruning tools**. Survives in **infected twigs,
fallen leaves and fruit on the orchard floor**. Managed by **removing and destroying infected plant
parts, disinfecting pruning tools between plants, controlling the bahar (forcing the rest period)
so that the susceptible flush avoids the monsoon, orchard sanitation, windbreaks, and copper
protectants**. The **bahar regulation** point is important and specific: shifting to **hasta bahar**
so that fruit development falls outside the heavy-rain window is a management decision made months
ahead. ⚠
