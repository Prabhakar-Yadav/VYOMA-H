### Life cycle and stage durations
- **Egg**: laid **singly, inserted inside the leaf veins** on the underside — so eggs are protected
  inside plant tissue and are invisible on the surface. **Incubation period 4–11 days.**
- **Nymph**: light green, translucent, found **between the veins on the under surface of leaves**.
  Nymphs walk characteristically **sideways (diagonally)** when disturbed — a reliable field
  identification cue. **Nymphal period 7–21 days**, with **five moults**.
- **Adult**: green, wedge-shaped, with a black spot on each forewing tip (hence *biguttula*,
  "two-spotted"). Adults jump and fly readily.
- **Total life cycle: 15–46 days. Eleven generations per year.**
(eagri ENTO 331 Lecture 10 — http://eagri.org/eagri50/ENTO331/lecture10/001.html)

### Where to look
Underside of leaves, in the **middle canopy**. Damage shows first at leaf margins.

### Damage and diagnosis
Both nymphs and adults suck sap from the under surface of leaves and inject toxic saliva. Tender
leaves turn yellow, **leaf margins curl downwards** (a downward cupping, distinct from the upward
cupping of thrips), then redden. Severe infestation gives a bronze or brick-red leaf — the classic
**"hopper burn"** — and growth is retarded.
(eagri ENTO 331 Lecture 10 — http://eagri.org/eagri50/ENTO331/lecture10/001.html)

### ETL
**50 nymphs/adults per 50 leaves**, i.e. an average of **1 per leaf**; or yellowing and curling
from the middle to the upper portion of the plant in **25 % of plants** in the field.
(eagri ENTO 331 Lecture 10)

### Host range and alternate hosts
Cotton, potato, brinjal, castor, okra (bhendi), tomato, hollyhock and sunflower. Okra is a
particularly important bridging host between cotton seasons, as is *Abutilon indicum*.

### Seasonal peak and weather
Peaks in the **rainy and immediately post-rainy period**, favoured by **moderate temperature with
high humidity**; populations are commonly highest **July to September**. Heavy driving rain
knocks nymphs off leaves. ⚠

### Off-season survival
Survives on okra, brinjal, castor, potato and malvaceous weeds through the non-cotton months.
There is no diapausing stage; survival depends on continuously available green hosts, so weed and
volunteer-host removal is the effective break.

### Spread
Strong adult flight and wind-assisted dispersal; immigration into a young crop from surrounding
okra and weed hosts is the usual source.

### Natural enemies in India
Green lacewing *Chrysoperla zastrowi sillemi* (cited in the TNAU package as *Chrysopa carnea*),
mymarid egg parasitoids *Anagrus* spp. and *Gonatocerus* spp. (they parasitise eggs inside the
vein), dryinid parasitoids, coccinellids, *Orius* spp., and spiders — especially lynx and jumping
spiders. Conserve by intercropping cotton with cowpea, greengram, blackgram or soybean, which
raises spider and coccinellid activity.
(eagri ENTO 331 Lecture 10 and Lecture 11 — http://eagri.org/eagri50/ENTO331/lecture11/003.html)

### Host-plant resistance
Leaf hairiness (pubescence) on the underside is the classical mechanism of jassid resistance in
cotton — hairy-leaved varieties carry far lower populations because the ovipositor and the nymphs
are physically obstructed. TNAU lists MCU 3, MCU 5 and MCU 9 as jassid-tolerant.
(eagri ENTO 331 Lecture 10)

### Virus transmission
No established plant virus is transmitted by *A. biguttula biguttula*. The damage is caused by
**toxicogenic feeding** — the saliva itself produces hopper burn.
